## 7.1. Checklist: correctness, performance, and maintainability

1. **Keep getters cheap and predictable** (no I/O; no locks unless you must; no surprising side effects)
2. **Enforce invariants** (validate at the boundary; keep objects valid after every mutation)
3. **Avoid representation leaks** (don’t expose mutable internals; prefer read-only views)
4. **Be explicit about thread-safety** (document it; synchronize or confine state; properties don’t “make it safe”)
5. **Prefer modern constructs** (`init`, `required`, immutability patterns) where they improve correctness

---

### 1) **Keep getters cheap and predictable**
**Definition**

A “good” property getter is:

- **Fast**: typically \(O(1)\), no allocations, no I/O.
- **Deterministic**: calling it twice in a row shouldn’t “do work” or produce surprising side effects.
- **Safe**: it should not throw in normal, expected states (exceptions are for contract violations / truly exceptional conditions).

**When to use**

- Use properties for **state** and **simple derived values**.
- Use methods for **operations** and **potentially expensive work** (`Compute…`, `Load…`, `Refresh…`, `Get…Async`).

**Common mistakes**

- Doing network/disk/DB work in a getter.
- Taking a lock in a getter “just in case” and accidentally turning a harmless read into a contention hotspot.
- Hiding allocations (e.g., `get => _list.ToArray();`) and causing GC churn.

**Performance note**

- If a getter needs synchronization, it’s usually a signal that the design has shared mutable state. See `README/CSharp/Cuncurrent & Parallel/README.md` for the deep treatment of synchronization and contention.

### 2) **Enforce invariants**
**Mentor rule**

- Invariants belong at **boundaries**: constructors/factories (initial validity) and methods/properties that mutate state (state transitions).
- Prefer **“valid-state-only”** objects: after any public operation, the object remains valid.

**Don’t duplicate full invariants theory here**

The canonical deep treatment of invariants/encapsulation is in:

- See `README/CSharp/Methods/sections/1. Method fundamentals/1.2. Encapsulation with methods invariants boundaries.md`.

**Practical checklist**

- Validate *inputs* before mutating state.
- Keep a single “source of truth” for validation (don’t copy/paste checks across multiple setters/methods).
- Use **Try-pattern** for expected failure (`TrySetX`, `TryParse`, `TryGetValue`) instead of exceptions.

### 3) **Avoid representation leaks**
Representation leaks happen when callers can mutate or depend on your internal state.

**Preferred patterns**

- Keep mutable data `private`.
- Expose **read-only views** (`IReadOnlyList<T>`, `IReadOnlyDictionary<TKey,TValue>`) or explicit snapshot methods when a stable view is required.

For the deep guide (including snapshots vs live views and span-based options), see:

- See `README/CSharp/Fields/sections/2. Accessibility and encapsulation for fields/2.2. Read-only exposure patterns.md`.

### 4) **Be explicit about thread-safety**
**Key point**

Properties don’t make your type thread-safe. They just provide syntax.

**Choose and document a policy**

- **Not thread-safe** (most domain models): callers must confine to one thread or synchronize externally.
- **Thread-safe for reads**: immutable after construction (best if possible).
- **Fully thread-safe**: internal synchronization + clear semantics for atomicity/consistency.

**Good to know**

- “Thread-safe” isn’t binary. Clarify whether *composite* operations are atomic (e.g., `Balance` and `LastUpdated` updated together).

Deep dive lives here:

- See `README/CSharp/Cuncurrent & Parallel/README.md`.

### 5) **Prefer modern constructs where they improve correctness**
Use modern language features when they **clarify intent** and reduce invalid states:

- **`init`** to prevent mutation after construction (good for DTOs/config; use carefully for domain invariants).
- **`required`** to enforce completeness at compile time for data carriers.
- **Records / `readonly`** patterns for value-like immutable state (with awareness of shallow vs deep immutability).
