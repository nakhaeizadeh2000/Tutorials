## 3.1. What the JIT sees (static calls, devirtualization, inlining realities)

1. **Call site shape** (extensions are static calls; compare to virtual/interface dispatch costs)
2. **Inlining** (small, stable helpers inline well; giant chained pipelines may not)
3. **Measure before “micro-optimizing”** (allocation and algorithm dominate more often than call overhead)

---

### 1. **Call site shape**

**Definition**: The JIT does not care that you *wrote* `x.Ext()`; what matters is what the C# compiler emitted. For extension methods, the call site is a **static call** where the receiver becomes the first argument (see section `1.1`).

**Why this matters**

- **Static call sites are straightforward**: easy to inline, easy to reason about, and do not incur virtual/interface dispatch by default.
- **Instance calls vary**:
  - **Non-virtual instance** calls can also be direct.
  - **Virtual/interface** calls may require dispatch; the JIT can sometimes devirtualize but it depends on type information and tiered compilation.

**Minimal example (mental model)**

```csharp
// You write:
var y = x.Frob();

// The compiler effectively emits:
var y2 = FrobExtensions.Frob(x);
```

**Mentor warning**

- Don’t “prefer extension methods for performance”. Extensions are just one *syntax* that becomes a static call.
- The real performance decisions are typically:
  - algorithm choice,
  - allocation behavior,
  - avoiding unnecessary async state machines,
  - and avoiding hot `IEnumerable<T>` pipelines.

---

### 2. **Inlining**

**Definition**: Inlining is when the JIT replaces a call with the callee’s body. Small, stable methods inline frequently; large/complex methods often don’t.

**What helps inlining (practical, not folklore)**

- Small body, simple control flow.
- No `try/catch` in the hot path.
- No large generic complexity explosion at the call site.
- The JIT can see the method body (cross-assembly inlining depends on the runtime, ReadyToRun/AOT mode, and other factors).

**Common mistake**

- Creating “fluent mega-extensions” that do a lot: they look nice, but they often:
  - don’t inline,
  - allocate,
  - hide expensive work,
  - and become hard to profile.

**Good pattern**: keep a thin extension that forwards to a focused helper.

```csharp
public static class IntExtensions
{
    public static bool IsEven(this int value) => (value & 1) == 0;
}
```

**Performance note**

- The win from inlining is often tiny compared to avoiding allocations or reducing work. Inlining helps most when:
  - the call is in a tight loop,
  - and the method is extremely small.

---

### 3. **Measure before “micro-optimizing”**

**Definition**: “Measure” means you use a profiler and/or microbenchmarking in a controlled environment, not intuition.

**Recommended practice**

- For microbenchmarks: [BenchmarkDotNet](https://benchmarkdotnet.org/) with release builds, warmups, and realistic inputs.
- For production: sample profilers (PerfView/dotnet-trace/dotnet-counters) and allocation tracking.

**Mentor checklist**

- **First**, find allocations (GC pressure) and high CPU methods.
- **Then**, reduce work (algorithm) before reducing “call overhead”.
- **Only then** consider low-level tactics (spans, pooling, ref structs) where it’s justified.

**Legacy/deprecated habit to avoid**

- “Extensions are slow” / “instance methods are slow” as blanket statements. On modern .NET, what matters is the *actual code* and its allocation/dispatch characteristics, not the surface syntax.
