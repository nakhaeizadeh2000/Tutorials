## 6.1. Alerts incidents severity priority mitigation and remediation

An alert says CPU is high. A support report says registration times out. These
are related investigation inputs, but the alert does not prove the user's failure
or its cause. Incident language should identify the observed impact, current
response and evidence for restoration.

This lesson uses a constructed operational incident. Security incidents have their
own classification context in the security domain. No alert, message or operational
change is sent or performed here.

### 1) **Separate a detection signal from an impact and response record**

An **alert** indicates that a defined condition needs attention. The condition may
be a symptom, resource warning or forecast. A **notification** delivers information
about it. **Paging** is an interrupting notification path to a responder, commonly
used for conditions requiring timely action. Not every recorded condition needs
to page a person; the rule and response purpose matter.

An **on-call** responder has assigned responsibility for responding during a stated
period. **Acknowledgment** records that a notification or assignment was received
under the tool's convention. It does not establish that the service recovered.
**Triage** assesses the situation, impact, urgency and next response route.

An **operational incident** is an unplanned disruption or degradation managed under
the team's incident process. A team may also declare an incident for a developing
threat to service before impact is fully known. State the known facts and local
criteria; an alert alone is not proof of a completed harmful event, and users may
discover an incident before an automated alert fires.

Google's original [incident management discussion](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)
emphasizes coordinated work, communication and a live account of the situation.
An **incident commander** coordinates the response and current priorities; operations
and communication responsibilities can be assigned separately. Small teams may
combine roles, but still need clear action ownership and handoff. A role name is
not a requirement to create a particular department or follow one vendor's workflow.

### 2) **Distinguish impact urgency relief and correction**

**Incident severity** describes impact under the organization's scale. Labels such
as **SEV1** or **SEV2** have no universal meaning or fixed number of levels. Define
affected operations, users, scope and uncertainty. The severity of a vulnerability
finding is also a different assessment from the severity of a live service incident.

**Priority** guides urgency and the order of response or work. Impact is an input,
but deadlines, active exposure, available workarounds and other responsibilities
can matter. A team may encode urgency in the same label or keep it separate.
Atlassian's primary [severity discussion](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management/kpis/severity-levels)
compares impact and urgency while making its example scale organization-specific.
Do not interpret every company's SEV2 as the same impact or response deadline.

**Mitigation** reduces or contains harmful impact. It might redirect work away from
an unhealthy instance or disable a problematic path. The earlier debugging lesson
owns its distinction from a workaround and a fault correction. **Remediation**
addresses an identified problem or contributing condition within a stated scope.
A proposed code or configuration change is not completed remediation until it is
implemented and relevant evidence supports the intended correction.

Mitigation can restore service while the deeper cause remains uncertain. Conversely,
a code correction does not automatically repair earlier missing or incorrect
business results. State what has been restored, what needs reconciliation and
which prevention work remains. An incident can be marked resolved under a team's
service-restoration rule while follow-up corrections remain open; resolution is
not automatically proof that every contributing factor was removed.

### 3) **Read a response timeline and preserve its unknowns**

The fictional service currently hosts workshops W1, W2 and W3. Its local rules say
SEV1 means confirmed registration failure across all active workshops; SEV2 means
confirmed partial registration disruption while some registration still works.
These are this packet's conventions, not a universal recommended scale.

| Time | Supplied record |
|---|---|
| 09:58 | CPU alert fires for API instance A after its configured threshold is crossed |
| 10:00 | On-call responder acknowledges the page |
| 10:02 | Five checked W1 registrations time out; checked W2 and W3 registrations succeed; team declares SEV2 and gives response immediate priority |
| 10:05 | Operations responder diverts W1 traffic from A to alternative B |
| 10:10 | The stated W1 registration read/write checks succeed through B; observed timeout impact for the checked path ends |
| 10:15 | Team closes the active incident under its restoration rule; earlier timed-out requests still need outcome reconciliation |
| Follow-up | Owner proposes investigating connection handling and correcting any confirmed fault; cause and correction remain unverified |

The CPU event is a detection signal. Acknowledgment at 10:00 is a response milestone.
The 10:02 records establish partial registration impact under the local SEV2 rule;
they do not establish that every user or every W1 request failed. The incident's
actual start is not established merely by the alert timestamp.

Diverting traffic is the mitigation action; the 10:10 checked outcomes support its
stated restoration scope. The CPU alert and improvement after diversion do not
uniquely identify a CPU defect, network problem or connection-management fault.
The follow-up hypothesis remains a hypothesis. The 10:15 status also does not
resolve whether earlier timed-out requests committed their writes.

A useful update says:

> Partial registration disruption is confirmed under our SEV2 rule. W1 traffic was
> diverted from A, and the stated checks through B now succeed. The active response
> is closed under our restoration rule. Cause investigation, a verified correction
> and the outcomes of earlier timed-out requests remain open with assigned owners.

Practice: remove the 10:10 checks, retaining only “routing change completed.” The
mitigation action remains recorded, but its effect on registration is unverified.
Report action complete and outcome awaiting verification instead of recovered.
Neither the page acknowledgment nor the command's success fills that gap.

### 4) **Common confusion**

Severity, priority and lifecycle status answer different questions: impact, urgency
and response progress. An acknowledged page can concern an unresolved incident;
a resolved operational response can still have open remediation and reconciliation.
Read the local workflow before equating its labels.

A CVSS score, a high CPU percentage and a local SEV number also describe different
subjects. Explain the observed user outcome rather than translating one number
mechanically into another scale or guessing a root cause.

### 5) **Performance note**

Alerts can involve latency, error fractions or resource pressure. More notifications
do not themselves improve service, and repeatedly interrupting responders can
consume attention without adding useful evidence. Mitigation may move load onto
remaining capacity, so verify the required workflow and its limits. No page-load,
response-speed, alert-quality, incident duration or service performance experiment
was run in this fictional timeline.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to mark signal, acknowledgment, impact, action, verified outcome
and proposed correction in separate timeline rows. A sound answer keeps cause and
old-request outcomes open after the incident status changes. Then remove the checks:
they should withdraw the supported restoration claim without erasing the recorded
action or inventing a failed mitigation.
