## 6.2. Runbooks playbooks postmortems and contributing factors

“Restart it, then mark resolved” leaves the responder to guess the target, authority,
expected result and failure path. An incident document should help someone make
and verify the appropriate decision, not merely contain familiar operational words.
Afterward, learning requires more than writing “operator error” in a report.

The preceding lesson owns response roles, mitigation and resolution. This page
reviews fictional instructions and incident records. It executes no procedure.

### 1) **Distinguish a task procedure from an investigation guide**

A **runbook** documents how to perform a defined task and check its outcome. It
should identify the intended result, applicable system/version, prerequisites,
needed tools and authority, steps, expected observations and what to do when the
procedure cannot continue safely. An owner and validation history help readers
know whether it still matches the current service and tools.

A **playbook** commonly guides investigation and response across possible situations.
It can branch on observations, identify escalation or communication paths and point
to a runbook once the appropriate action is selected. AWS's primary explanations
use [runbooks for procedures](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/ops_ready_to_support_use_runbooks.html)
and [playbooks for investigation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/ops_ready_to_support_use_playbooks.html).
Organizations sometimes use the words differently. Inspect the actual purpose
and contents instead of rejecting a useful document over its title.

A runbook can be manual, automated or a combination. Automation does not establish
that the procedure targets the right resource, handles current failures or verifies
the user's operation. Likewise, a prose document does not become a reliable procedure
merely because someone copied it into version control. Validate relevant actions
and outcomes in the actual supported environment before relying on it operationally.

Review this **constructed instruction**:

> Restart it, wait until green, and close the incident.

A useful rewrite first defines what the real procedure must supply:

| Missing decision or evidence | Requirement for the revised document |
|---|---|
| What is it? | Identify the service, environment, resource and applicable version |
| When is restart appropriate? | State prerequisites and observations selecting this action; otherwise route investigation or escalation |
| Who may act, using which steps? | State the authorized role, supported tool and exact target-specific procedure |
| What does green mean? | Name the required workflow checks and acceptable outcomes, not only a process-health signal |
| What if the action or check fails? | Give stop conditions, the supported fallback and an escalation route |
| What should be recorded? | Record actions, observed outcomes, relevant identifiers and unresolved work without secrets |

This table is a **review specification**, not a complete executable restart runbook.
It intentionally does not invent a safe command for an unknown system. A real author
must fill the task-specific steps and verify them. The lesson's outcome is identifying
those missing decisions and evidence, not pretending a generic restart is validated.

### 2) **Use a postmortem to explain the event and its contributing conditions**

A **postmortem** is a retrospective account of an incident and learning from it.
Teams may use names such as **post-incident review** or **after-action review**.
The document commonly records impact, timeline, response, contributing conditions,
uncertainties and follow-up work. It differs from a live status update, which helps
coordinate the current response. Google's original [postmortem discussion](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)
connects the account to preventive actions and review rather than a forgotten form.

A **blameless postmortem** investigates what people did, what they knew and why
choices made sense in context without reducing the explanation to personal blame.
It still records actions, responsibilities and consequences. John Allspaw's original
[Etsy account](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems)
connects learning with situational information and accountability. Avoiding blame
does not mean deleting uncomfortable facts or declaring that all behavior is acceptable.

The earlier debugging owner explains root-cause scope and **contributing factors**.
A trigger can start an event while other conditions determine its impact. Missing
capacity evidence, ambiguous instructions or delayed detection may matter alongside
a code or configuration fault. Identify which explanations the evidence supports
and which remain hypotheses. The term RCA, for root-cause analysis, does not require
inventing one deepest cause for every complex event.

**Hindsight bias** means evaluating an earlier decision as if the eventual outcome
had been obvious with the information available then. Compare the actual evidence
and options at the time. “Be more careful” supplies neither a mechanism nor a check
that the same confusing situation will be handled differently next time. Training
can be useful when a specific knowledge gap is established, but it is not a substitute
for examining tools, constraints and the system's behavior.

### 3) **Turn an incident account into owned verifiable follow-up**

Read this **fictional evidence packet**:

| Record | Supplied fact |
|---|---|
| Change | Configuration R8 doubles worker concurrency; deployment uses the documented procedure |
| Precheck | The recorded check validates syntax; it does not exercise database load or capacity limits |
| 12:00 | Responder applies R8 after seeing the syntax check pass |
| 12:01–12:03 | Recorded database in-flight work rises; user-visible failures then appear |
| 12:05 | Team restores the prior concurrency configuration |
| 12:08 | The stated user-workflow checks succeed again |
| Review note | Operator should have known better; prevent recurrence by telling everyone to be careful |

The facts support a temporal account and a response result. They also reveal that
the precheck did not test the proposed capacity claim. Increased concurrency and
load are a useful hypothesis to investigate, but the packet does not uniquely
identify every database or application mechanism involved. Do not turn sequence
alone into a complete causal proof.

A stronger review note says:

> R8 increased concurrency after a syntax-only check. Database in-flight work rose
> and user failures followed; the prior configuration was restored and the stated
> workflow checks recovered. Investigate the workload and database limits, and
> document which pre-deployment evidence is required for concurrency changes.
> Keep the detailed failure mechanism open until evidence supports it.

An **action item** is follow-up work with a defined owner and expected outcome.
Give it enough acceptance evidence to distinguish planned, implemented and verified
work. For this packet, a proposed item might assign the application and data owners
to define the supported concurrency envelope, test a representative workload and
update the procedure's prechecks. Acceptance requires recorded workload, settings,
outcomes and a check demonstrating the documented boundary. This proposal is still
planned; no test or procedure update has occurred in this exercise.

Practice: change the action tracker from planned to done because the revised document
was uploaded, while the required workload check remains unrun. The document-publication
part may be complete, but the item's stated verification condition is not. Report
the remaining check explicitly rather than treating one activity as the full outcome.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A runbook explains a task; a playbook often helps select an investigation or task.
Names vary, so agree on local usage. A postmortem explains a past event and learning;
it is not proof that every proposed prevention measure was implemented.

Blameless does not mean actionless or factless. Keep the actual actions and available
information visible while examining the conditions that shaped them. A completed
incident response also does not imply completed postmortem follow-up.

### 5) **Performance note**

Clear procedures and retained evidence can reduce repeated investigation work, but
a document's existence does not establish a measured response-time improvement.
A concurrency change can increase downstream pressure; a validation step needs the
actual workload and cost evidence. No procedure run, database load test, incident
reproduction or response-efficiency measurement was performed here.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask a learner to annotate the restart instruction with its missing target, selecting
condition, verification and fallback. Then give the R8 packet and ask what the responder
actually knew at 12:00. A strong answer preserves the action, avoids hindsight blame,
marks the causal hypothesis and writes an owned item whose completion can be checked.
Test that judgment by revealing an uploaded document with an unrun acceptance check.
