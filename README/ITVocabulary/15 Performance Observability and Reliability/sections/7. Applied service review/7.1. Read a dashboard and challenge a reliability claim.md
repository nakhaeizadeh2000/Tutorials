## 7.1. Read a dashboard and challenge a reliability claim

A green dashboard is a set of selected views, not an independent guarantee that
users can complete their work. Review each panel's operation, query, population,
time window and calculation, then compare it with the stated service objective.

This is a cross-topic reading exercise. Every chart value, query description and
recovery record below is fictional. No monitoring backend or service is queried.

### 1) **Recover the intended service claim and each panel’s actual scope**

A **dashboard** presents selected observations and computed views, often as panels.
A title such as availability can conceal a narrower query. Read the query meaning
and data coverage rather than assigning the panel's number to the whole service.

This team defines its objective as:

> At least 99.9% of eligible received registration requests across regions R1 and
> R2 return correct results within 500 ms during the fixed 24-hour reporting window.

The objective's API-received boundary is explicit. Requests that never reach it
need other evidence if the team wants to assess the complete browser experience.
The primary [SLI implementation discussion](https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/)
explains why one intended outcome can have measurement implementations with different
coverage. Apply that distinction to this packet rather than changing the objective
silently to match an easy chart.

Read the **constructed dashboard and supporting report**:

| Item | Actual query or record |
|---|---|
| Availability: 99.99% | 9,999 successful health-route probes out of 10,000; R1 only; the same 24-hour window |
| Average response: 40 ms | Arithmetic mean of successful registration responses in R1 during the last ten minutes; timeouts and R2 excluded |
| Worker A p99: 100 ms | Its 900 included observations during the last ten minutes |
| Worker B p99: 2,000 ms | Its 100 included observations during the same ten minutes; populations are disjoint |
| Global p99: 1,050 ms | The panel averages the two worker p99 values |
| Direct objective report | Complete classification of 200,000 eligible API-received registrations in both regions for the 24-hour window: 199,500 good and 500 bad under the stated rule |

Google's original [monitoring discussion](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
contrasts request timing, error outcomes and misleading aggregate centers. Here,
the health panel and the success-only mean retain useful information, but neither
is the full registration SLI.

### 2) **Separate a known objective miss from unsupported broader claims**

The direct objective report yields 199,500 / 200,000 = 99.75% good registrations.
That misses the stated 99.9% target. The allowed bad-event count is 0.001 × 200,000
= 200; the 500 observed bad events exceed it by 300. These calculations use the
supplied complete classifications for the objective's own boundary and window.
They do not identify which region, cause or individual customer accounts for the
bad events.

The health panel's arithmetic is also valid: 9,999 / 10,000 = 99.99%. The problem
is assigning it to another operation and population. Its health route and R1 scope
do not establish correct registration in both regions. Do not call the underlying
probe result false simply because its broad dashboard title is misleading.

The 40 ms mean likewise does not establish a service-wide p99, include timeouts or
cover R2. A favorable successful-response center can coexist with slow or failed
requests. Ask for the intended population and distribution when investigating the
registration objective, while retaining the mean's actual scope.

The global-p99 panel has an aggregation problem. Averaging 100 and 2,000 gives
1,050, but that is an average of summaries, not the combined population's p99.
Request counts do not repair the missing distribution: weighting those two p99s
is not generally a pooled percentile either. The percentile owner demonstrates
why. With only these summaries, the exact pooled p99 remains unestablished.

Now read a **separate recovery rehearsal card**, not an outage measured by the
24-hour registration report:

| Recovery item | Supplied record |
|---|---|
| RTO | Required registration workflow restored within 20 minutes of interruption |
| RPO | Complete recovered history no older than 5 minutes before interruption |
| At minute 8 | Alternative environment's health route responds |
| At minute 22 | Required identity, registration reads and writes are validated and restored |
| Recovered history | Complete through 2 minutes before interruption |

The rehearsal meets its stated RPO but misses its RTO: 22 minutes exceeds 20. The
8-minute health milestone is a narrower observation. It cannot replace the required
workflow finish, and the card does not establish production recovery for all future
failures or loads. Keep the separate rehearsal and live-report scopes visible.

### 3) **Write an evidence-based correction and choose the next question**

A technical review could say:

> The intended 24-hour registration SLI is 99.75%, below 99.9%, with 500 bad events
> against an allowance of 200. The 99.99% panel describes R1 health probes. The
> 40 ms panel excludes R2 and timeouts. The global-p99 calculation needs a pooled
> distribution rather than averaged worker quantiles. The separate rehearsal's
> recovered data meets its five-minute point objective, but its 22-minute workflow
> restoration misses the 20-minute time objective. We still need the breakdown of
> bad registrations, their relevant causes and owned follow-up.

A product-facing update could say:

> The broad green labels overstate what was checked. The direct registration report
> misses the agreed target, while the health checks cover a narrower path. The
> recovery rehearsal recovered sufficiently recent data but restored the complete
> workflow later than its target. We are separating the failed objectives from
> the remaining questions about affected users, causes and corrective work.

Practice: remove the direct objective report, leaving only the dashboard panels.
The 99.75% calculation and established SLO miss must now be withdrawn because their
source counts are absent. The panels still fail to establish that the objective
was met. “Not established” and “known to be missed” are different conclusions.
Request the matching indicator data instead of guessing either result.

In an interview, ask whether more successful health probes would settle the
registration claim. A sound answer says the issue is the measured operation and
scope, not simply the number of probe samples. Better precision on the wrong
question does not answer the intended one.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A valid number can support an invalid broader claim. The health ratio is valid
for its probes; the pooled-p99 claim is not established by its calculation. Diagnose
the specific problem instead of dismissing every panel or trusting every green one.

A missed objective is also not a unique cause. This packet establishes the miss
from the direct report, but no database, deployment or network cause follows from
a ratio alone. Keep investigation and verification questions attached to their owners.

### 5) **Performance note**

Filtering and aggregation can make queries cheaper or easier to read while hiding
important populations. Retaining detailed evidence has cost, so select representations
that answer the actual question. No dashboard query cost, service latency, live
availability, recovery duration or improvement was measured here; the exercise
checks supplied counts and interpretation only.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to mark every panel with operation, region, outcomes and window.
Then require two conclusions: one known failed objective and one unsupported claim.
A strong answer calculates the direct result, rejects averaged quantiles and keeps
RTO separate from RPO. Remove the direct report to test whether they appropriately
reduce certainty without turning the remaining green panels into proof of success.
