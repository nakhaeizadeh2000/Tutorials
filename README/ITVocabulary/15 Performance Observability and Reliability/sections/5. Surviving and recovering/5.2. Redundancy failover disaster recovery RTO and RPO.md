## 5.2. Redundancy failover disaster recovery RTO and RPO

A standby can be running while a usable recovery remains unverified. A restore
can also finish quickly while recovering data that is too old. Separate spare
resources, switching service, restoring acceptable operation and preserving recent
accepted history.

The data domain owns backup, replication and restore mechanisms. This lesson
applies them to service recovery objectives. Its timeline is fictional; no system
is switched, restored or tested here.

### 1) **Distinguish extra components from a working recovery path**

**Redundancy** supplies additional components, capacity or information for a defined
purpose. Two application instances can provide alternatives, but shared power,
hardware, credentials, data or faulty configuration can still affect both. State
which failure boundary the alternatives separate and whether the surviving capacity
can handle the required work.

A **failover** changes service to another participant or environment. It can be
automatic or operator-controlled. Detection, routing, access, data readiness and
available capacity affect whether users actually regain the required operation.
A completed DNS or load-balancer change is one step, not a complete recovery result.
**Failback** returns service to an intended primary environment after recovery;
it needs its own data and operational coordination.

In an **active-passive** arrangement, one side normally serves the stated workload
while another is prepared for takeover. In **active-active**, multiple sides serve
work concurrently under defined routing and state rules. The labels alone do not
establish current data, independent failures or enough capacity after one side is
lost. Preventing conflicting write authority remains a distributed-systems question.

**Disaster recovery**, or **DR**, plans and performs recovery from substantial
service disruption. The disaster can involve infrastructure, software, accidental
changes or attack; it need not be a natural disaster or complete regional loss.
Its required service, data, people, access and dependencies extend beyond having
a backup file. The preceding properties lesson owns the distinction between
continuing through a fault and recovering after interruption.

Read recovery strategy names through the work remaining:

| Strategy name | Typical readiness idea | Remaining question |
|---|---|---|
| Backup and restore | Retained recovery material, with systems restored or provisioned as needed | Can the full application become usable within its target? |
| Pilot light | Selected core resources maintained; other parts activated or created for recovery | Which parts are absent, and how are they made ready? |
| Warm standby | A functional alternative maintained at reduced capacity | How does it obtain required capacity and current compatible state? |
| Hot standby | Alternative prepared at the stated full serving capacity | Are routing, data, access and failure assumptions actually verified? |

Seth Eliot's original [pilot-light and warm-standby explanation](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/disaster-recovery-dr-architecture-on-aws-part-iii-pilot-light-and-warm-standby/)
contrasts readiness and retained-data needs. The terminology describes an arrangement,
not guaranteed recovery minutes. A standby can also replicate an unwanted deletion;
retained history and a usable restoration path remain separate concerns.

### 2) **Define recovery time and data-point objectives separately**

A **recovery time objective**, or **RTO**, is the maximum acceptable time to restore
the defined service after interruption under the organization's rule. Specify the
start event and acceptable finish. A server booting, a health endpoint answering
and the complete required workflow functioning are different possible milestones.
Choose the milestone matching the objective rather than the earliest green signal.

A **recovery point objective**, or **RPO**, specifies how recent the recovered data
must be relative to the interruption. It is commonly expressed as a maximum
acceptable time gap between the recovery point and that interruption. RPO 5 minutes
requires an appropriate recoverable state within that bound, not a five-minute
restore duration. AWS's primary [DR objective definitions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/reliability-pillar/disaster-recovery-dr-objectives.html)
separate these two axes and distinguish an RTO target from an average recovery metric.

An objective states an acceptable outcome. A measured recovery duration or recovered
point states what a particular exercise or event established. “RTO 30 minutes” is
not evidence that restoration has been completed in 30 minutes. Similarly, a
scheduled five-minute backup interval does not prove RPO 5: failed captures,
incomplete contents and unavailable log history can leave an older usable point.

Evaluate the accepted operations and consistent history at the recovered point.
A restored database may require replay, application compatibility, credentials,
keys and access before the service can operate. Link those steps to their actual
owners instead of treating one job's success flag as the full result.

### 3) **Compare a recovery timeline with both objectives**

Use this **constructed rehearsal packet**, with all timestamps on the same clock:

| Item | Supplied requirement or observation |
|---|---|
| Required recovery | Correct registration reads and writes through the required identity and data path |
| RTO | At most 30 minutes from service interruption to that usable workflow |
| RPO | At most 5 minutes between complete recovered history and interruption |
| Interruption begins | 09:20 |
| Latest usable recovered data point | Complete committed history through 09:12; no later usable recovery history |
| Confirmed missing data | A registration acknowledged at 09:14 is absent from the recovered state |
| Health route answers | 09:35 |
| Required workflow validated and restored | 09:55 |

The health route answers after 15 minutes, but the stated recovery milestone is
09:55. The recovery duration is 35 minutes, exceeding the 30-minute RTO. Reporting
15 minutes would substitute a narrower milestone for the required outcome.

The recovered history ends 8 minutes before interruption, exceeding the 5-minute
RPO gap. The missing 09:14 acknowledgment confirms that the recovered state omits
an accepted operation from more than 5 minutes before interruption. The timestamps
do not establish how many records were lost in every minute of the gap. An 8-minute
gap is a time boundary, not eight rows or a measured data-volume percentage.

Practice: change only the usable recovery history so it now includes every committed
operation through 09:18, including the 09:14 registration. Keep the workflow recovery
time at 09:55. The data gap becomes 2 minutes and meets the stated RPO; recovery
still takes 35 minutes and misses RTO. Improving one axis does not automatically
improve the other.

A useful handoff states both outcomes, the required workflow and the missing
assumptions for other failures. This rehearsal's result does not establish every
future recovery, all traffic volumes or the ability to fail back safely. Record
what happened and what remains untested before choosing further work.

### 4) **Common confusion**

RTO is a time-to-usable-service objective; RPO concerns how far back the recovered
data point may be. Neither is a guarantee supplied by the words backup, standby
or multi-region. The actual recovery chain and its evidence matter.

Failover also does not imply zero interruption or zero data loss. A new destination
can respond while lacking required data or capacity. Recovery must check the service
and data outcomes that the organization actually needs.

### 5) **Performance note**

Keeping prepared capacity and replicated state costs resources; activating,
transferring, replaying and validating during recovery also takes work. Strategy
names alone supply no fixed latency, price or recovery-duration ranking for every
system. This packet checks elapsed-time arithmetic only. No failover, capacity,
backup/restore, replication lag, loss or recovery-time experiment was run.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to mark the interruption, health and usable-workflow milestones
before calculating RTO. Then ask what the missing 09:14 record adds to the data
claim. A sound answer distinguishes complete-history age from record counts and
updates only RPO when the recovered point advances to 09:18. Finish by asking what
would need verification before returning service to the original environment.
