## 2.1. Benchmarks baselines profiles bottlenecks and capacity

A benchmark says a parser is twice as fast. A CPU profile shows that parser almost
everywhere. Neither statement alone establishes that registration requests finish
twice as fast. The benchmark's task, the profile's resource and the user's complete
path need to line up.

Use the preceding timing and distribution vocabulary. The packets below are
fictional and the arithmetic is a model, not an observed benchmark or profile.

### 1) **Define the comparison before interpreting a benchmark score**

A **benchmark** is a defined workload and measurement procedure used to evaluate
performance. People also call its result a benchmark; distinguish the procedure
from the reported number. A **workload** describes the work being applied, including
operation mix, inputs, data size, arrival pattern and relevant concurrency.

A **baseline** is the reference configuration or observation used for comparison.
It might be the current release, a known reference implementation or a recorded
run. Identify its version and conditions. “Faster than baseline” is incomplete
without the metric, amount, units and comparable candidate conditions.

A **microbenchmark** isolates a small operation. An application benchmark exercises
a wider path. Neither is automatically valid or invalid: choose the boundary from
the question. A parser microbenchmark can investigate parsing; it cannot certify
database-backed registration throughput. Do not silently replace the user task
with an easier operation because the tool can measure it conveniently.

Brendan Gregg's original [active benchmarking guide](https://www.brendangregg.com/activebenchmarking.html)
recommends examining the system while the benchmark runs to understand what limits
it. A benchmark client can itself be the limiter. Inputs, runtime settings, caches,
resource limits and competing work can also change the comparison. A neat chart
and many repeats do not repair a procedure that measures the wrong task.

Keep correctness and failures beside performance. A candidate that omits validation
or returns errors quickly may improve a timing counter while failing the required
operation. Preserve the intended behavior and report outcome populations. Choose
measurement duration and repetitions to investigate the relevant behavior and
variation; there is no universal fixed run count for every benchmark.

### 2) **Use profiles to investigate a limiting path**

A **performance profile** attributes measured activity or cost to parts of an
execution. A **sampling profiler** periodically observes execution state rather
than recording every call. A **CPU profile** focuses on active CPU work. Other
profiles examine allocation, retained memory, blocking or different resources.
Go's primary [diagnostics guide](https://go.dev/doc/diagnostics) gives concrete
examples of CPU, heap and blocking profiles; their scopes differ.

A **hotspot** is a part contributing substantial activity under the chosen profile
and workload. It is a useful investigation target, not automatically a defect or
the largest part of user response time. CPU samples do not account for waiting
for remote I/O in the same way as an elapsed-time trace. Read the profiler's actual
sampling and attribution rules, including self versus descendant activity.

A **flame graph** can group sampled stacks to show where the selected activity is
concentrated. Read its units and legend before treating width as wall time or its
horizontal order as a timeline. Full JavaScript profiling mechanics stay with the
linked language owner.

A **bottleneck** limits a particular workload's performance under specified
conditions. It may be a resource, software limit, serialized step or dependency.
Changing the workload can move the bottleneck. A CPU hotspot can be worth improving
without being the dominant limit on the complete request. Conversely, little CPU
activity does not exclude a serious waiting-time problem.

Consider an explicit **serial response-time model**: a request waits 80 ms for a
DB operation, then parses its result for 20 ms, with no overlap or other cost.
Total time is 100 ms. If all CPU work is parsing, a CPU profile could be entirely
parser activity while parsing still represents only 20% of this modeled elapsed
path. Halving parsing changes 20 to 10 ms, so total time becomes 90 ms: a 10% time
reduction, not 50%. Eliminating parsing altogether would still leave 80 ms under
these assumptions. This arithmetic is not a measured prediction for a real service.

### 3) **Read a capacity report against its workload and criteria**

**Service capacity** is the workload a stated configuration can handle while
meeting specified criteria. It differs from a hardware storage amount and from
throughput observed at an arbitrary operating point. Define the workload and what
acceptable means before calling a number capacity.

Read this **constructed test report**. Three independent ten-minute runs use the
same configuration and registration workload mix. The stated arrival rates are
received by the service; all arrivals are admitted and counted. Completion counts
include success and failure responses. The local acceptance criteria are p95 at
most 200 ms, at most 1% failed completions, and no growing unfinished backlog.

| Received arrivals per second | Completed responses per second | p95 response time | Failed completions | Unfinished backlog |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 20 | 100 ms | 0% | Stable |
| 40 | 40 | 180 ms | 0% | Stable |
| 60 | 42 | 900 ms | 10% | Growing |

The highest tested point meeting all stated criteria is 40 arrivals/second. The
60/second point fails the latency, outcome and backlog criteria even though it
completes slightly more responses. The exact maximum qualifying arrival rate has
not been established: intermediate points, longer operation or another workload
were not tested in this packet.

A capacity plan additionally considers expected demand, changes, headroom and
failure conditions. Passing 40/second with all instances present does not establish
40/second after losing an instance. A request-count target also needs its operation
mix: 40 small lookups can differ from 40 large exports. Do not turn one tested
throughput into a permanent guarantee for every route or data size.

Practice: a second report claims the new release is faster because its p95 is
120 ms, but it used 20 arrivals/second while the baseline's 180 ms used 40. That
comparison does not isolate the release change. Request comparable workloads and
settings, outcome evidence and variation before attributing the difference to
code. The candidate might be better; these two numbers do not establish it.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A baseline is a comparison reference, not automatically an acceptable target.
A profile helps attribute activity, while a benchmark measures a defined task;
a capacity claim adds workload and acceptable-outcome conditions.

“Twice as fast” also needs a quantity. Halving a helper's time does not halve a
complete serial path unless that helper accounts for the whole path. Increasing
responses per second can coexist with failures and an accumulating backlog.

### 5) **Performance note**

Profiling and measurement themselves consume resources and can perturb the result.
Select the needed evidence and assess its overhead rather than assuming a fixed
percentage. The 100→90 ms model explains a possible bound under its assumptions;
no parser speedup, CPU profile, benchmark-client limit or service capacity was
measured here. Existing language owners explain concrete measurement tools.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Show “100% of CPU samples are parsing” before revealing the 80 ms DB wait. Ask the
learner to predict the effect of halving parsing, then justify 90 ms from the model.
For the capacity table, ask why 42 completed responses/second is not the accepted
capacity point. A strong answer preserves the outcome, backlog and workload criteria
and identifies the missing comparison instead of choosing the largest number.
