## 2.2. Cold starts warmups caches hits misses TTLs and eviction

The first request after a restart takes longer than later requests. That observation
could involve initialization, connection setup, code optimization or an empty cache.
Calling it a cold start does not identify which work occurred or prove that every
later request will be fast.

The storage domain owns cache roles, authority, expiration and eviction. This page
adds state-dependent performance interpretation. All timelines and costs below
are constructed; no runtime startup or cache service is measured.

### 1) **Identify which execution state is cold or warm**

A **cold start** involves work needed to make an execution context ready, such as
loading code, initializing application state or establishing resources. Its precise
scope is platform-specific. For a hosted function, distinguish provider environment
preparation from application initialization and the request's own work. The earlier
serverless lesson owns the provider and invocation vocabulary.

**Warmup** is a period in which relevant execution state develops before a chosen
measurement or operating condition. Code optimization, cache population and
connection establishment can have different warmup behavior. A **warm execution**
reuses specified prepared state; it does not guarantee unchanged load, data, process
lifetime or response time.

Suppose an initial request takes 120 ms and a later one takes 20 ms after both
connections and a local cache have been populated. The later observation is faster
in this packet. It does not isolate a 100 ms code-optimization benefit: several
conditions changed. Record the state and measure the relevant boundaries rather
than assign all of the difference to a just-in-time compiler.

Do not remove startup observations automatically. If the user task is the first
request after deployment or a fresh process, startup belongs to that task. If the
question is sustained behavior after preparation, describe and verify the prepared
state. A service can also return to cold conditions through replacement, eviction
or changes in what users request.

### 2) **Distinguish a usable cache result from a current source fact**

A **cache hit** means the lookup supplies a reusable cached result under the cache's
rules. A **cache miss** means it does not. A miss may require fetching or computing
a result and possibly retaining it as a **refill**. The source can fail; a miss does
not guarantee successful refill. Also distinguish a missing entry from a lookup
error according to the system's reporting convention.

A **cache hit ratio** divides counted hits by the defined lookup population. If
100 classified lookups contain 90 hits and 10 misses, the hit ratio is 90%. That
is not 90% of all application requests unless every request makes exactly one of
those counted lookups. Multiple cache layers or lookups per request change that
relationship. A hit also need not mean a positive business answer: a cache may
retain a result saying that an item was not found.

A **time to live**, or **TTL**, sets a lifetime under a defined rule. State when it
starts and whether particular operations extend it. TTL expiration is different
from capacity-driven eviction. Redis's primary [expiration reference](https://redis.io/docs/latest/commands/expire/)
and [eviction guide](https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/reference/eviction/)
illustrate distinct expiration controls and configurable memory policies. They do
not imply one universal retention policy for every cache. An entry may disappear
before its TTL because of eviction or another removal.

A retained value can also be older than the authoritative source while still
eligible for reuse under the stated TTL policy. **Invalidation** makes a cached
entry cease to qualify after a relevant change, rather than relying only on its
remaining lifetime. State the acceptable age and change behavior for the task.
Neither persistence nor a high hit ratio establishes that a permission decision
or booking availability is current enough.

The original AWS [caching challenges discussion](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/caching-challenges-and-strategies/)
connects cache state, expiration, downstream load and operational failures. One
risk is a **cache stampede**, also called a **thundering herd**: many requests miss
for the same needed result and initiate overlapping refills. An empty cache or
shared expiration can therefore shift load onto the source. Coordinating refills,
limiting admitted work or choosing another policy involves trade-offs; this term
does not prescribe one universal implementation.

### 3) **Trace freshness lifetime and cost through explicit policies**

Use this **fictional cache policy**: inserting V1 at time 0 gives it a fixed expiry
at time 10 seconds. Reads do not extend expiry. The cache is used for a display
whose policy permits this bounded reuse; it is not authoritative for new bookings.

| Time | Supplied event | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | V1 copied from the source into the cache | Entry starts its stated lifetime |
| 4 | Source changes to V2; no invalidation is sent | Cached V1 does not update automatically |
| 5 | Lookup returns cached V1 under the policy | Hit; permitted reuse does not mean latest source value |
| 10 | Lookup finds the entry no longer reusable and fetches V2 successfully | Miss followed by a successful refill in this packet |

Now change only one condition: at time 6, a memory-pressure policy evicts V1. A
lookup at time 7 can miss even though the original lifetime would have lasted until
10. The trigger is capacity policy, not evidence that the TTL was ignored or that
the source record disappeared.

For a separate **serial cost model**, a direct source read costs 9 ms. Adding a
cache lookup costs 1 ms per lookup; a hit needs no source read, and a miss adds the
same 9 ms source read. Ignore refill writes and every other cost for this model.
Ten direct reads cost 90 ms in total. With 9 hits and 1 miss, the total is
9 × 1 + 1 × (1 + 9) = 19 ms. With 10 misses, it is 100 ms, more than the direct
path. These are sums of hypothetical operation costs, not wall time for concurrent
requests, measured speedups or costs of a particular cache product.

Practice: replace the time-5 response observation with “the cache process is warm.”
Can the report still establish a hit for this key? No. A running process may hold
other keys while this one is absent, expired or inaccessible. Request the lookup
result and its policy before reporting a hit or a freshness outcome.

### 4) **Common confusion**

Cold and warm need a subject. A reused process can have an empty application cache;
a new process can use an already populated external cache. Neither state alone
establishes the readiness of every dependency or code path.

A hit is also not a source-currentness test. TTL permits reuse under a policy; it
does not observe all source changes. Conversely, early eviction removes a cached
copy without necessarily losing the authoritative record.

### 5) **Performance note**

Lookup, serialization, transfer, refill and coordination all contribute costs.
A favorable hit ratio can coexist with expensive misses, stale results or a source
load spike after expiration. Use the actual workload and required behavior, including
startup where relevant. Only the stated arithmetic is verified here; no cold-start,
warmup, Redis, stampede, cache speed or memory-pressure benchmark was run.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask whether V1 at time 5 is a cache hit, the latest source value and acceptable for
the stated display. The three answers are yes, no and yes under the given policy.
Change the task to one requiring a source-current booking decision and ask which
assumption needs review. Then show the all-miss cost model: a sound explanation
accounts for the extra lookup instead of claiming caches always reduce work.
