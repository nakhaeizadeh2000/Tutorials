## 4.1. Timeouts retries backoff jitter and circuit breakers

A slow call leaves two questions: how long should this caller wait, and what is
safe to do next? A timeout answers the waiting question at a specified boundary.
It does not establish whether the remote operation changed anything.

Read the earlier network-timeout and idempotency lessons. This lesson adds retry
schedules and protection decisions. All timings and breaker states below are
constructed examples, not measured service behavior or production settings.

### 1) **Name the waiting limit and preserve an unknown outcome**

A **timeout** limits the duration spent waiting for a specified operation. A
**deadline** identifies the point after which a caller is unwilling to wait.
Connection establishment, response headers, an idle interval and the complete
operation can have different limits. A timeout option's name does not establish
which stages it covers.

The [gRPC deadline guide](https://grpc.io/docs/guides/deadlines/) distinguishes
point-in-time deadlines from duration limits and explains propagation to downstream
calls. It also distinguishes cancellation of the call from stopping all work the
application started. A cancellation signal does not undo an already committed
booking or prove that every worker noticed the signal.

Consider this original trace on a relative elapsed-time axis:

| Elapsed time | Observation |
|---|---|
| 0 ms | Client starts one booking request |
| 80 ms | Server records the booking successfully |
| 100 ms | Client's response-wait deadline expires without a response |
| 120 ms | A diagnostic record later establishes the server's earlier success |

At 100 ms, the client's business outcome is unknown. At 120 ms, the added evidence
establishes a recorded booking. The timeout did not roll it back. If the server
record is absent from the evidence packet, retain the uncertainty; absence of
that evidence is not proof of absence of the booking.

A whole-operation budget must account for time already spent. Suppose the caller
allows 1,000 ms in total and preparation consumes 250 ms. Only 750 ms remains for
downstream attempts, waits and finishing work. Giving each subsequent call another
fresh 1,000 ms does not preserve the original budget. The exact cancellation and
cleanup behavior still depends on the API and application.

### 2) **Bound repeated attempts and distinguish backoff from jitter**

A **retry** makes another attempt at an operation. Distinguish total attempts from
additional retries: one initial attempt plus two retries means three attempts.
Retry a failure only when another attempt is useful and safe under the contract.
Repeated malformed input is not repaired by waiting; an uncertain write needs its
operation-identity and duplicate-effect rules checked first.

**Backoff** inserts a delay before another attempt. **Exponential backoff** grows
that delay multiplicatively; **capped backoff** limits its growth. Here is one
fictional schedule, doubling from 100 ms with an 800 ms cap:

| Additional retry | Delay ceiling before it | One illustrative jittered delay |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 ms | 40 ms |
| 2 | 200 ms | 170 ms |
| 3 | 400 ms | 110 ms |
| 4 | 800 ms | 610 ms |
| 5 | 800 ms | 30 ms |

**Jitter** introduces variation into scheduling so clients do not all retry at
the same instant. In this particular full-jitter model, each chosen delay lies
between zero and its current ceiling. A selected delay can decrease even while
the ceiling grows. These hand-picked values illustrate the allowed range; they
are not random samples or a prescribed algorithm for every client.

Marc Brooker's [original simulation and explanation](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/)
shows why identical backed-off schedules can still form bursts and compares jitter
strategies. Its simulation results are workload-specific, not measurements of
this booking system. Jitter changes timing; it does not make duplicate effects safe.

The five ceilings total 2,300 ms before counting request time. Even the illustrative
chosen delays total 960 ms. After the earlier 250 ms preparation, that schedule
would exceed a 1,000 ms whole-operation budget before request durations are added.
A **retry budget** bounds repeated attempts or work under an explicit time or
capacity allowance; state which resource it limits. A real policy must stop or
decline attempts when its remaining budget is
insufficient; a maximum attempt count alone does not establish a time bound.

Also identify which layer retries. If three nested call layers each make up to
three total attempts per invocation, one outer request can produce up to 27
innermost attempts in the constructed all-failing case. AWS's
[first-hand retry discussion](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/timeouts-retries-and-backoff-with-jitter/)
explains amplification and overload risks. Coordinate retry ownership instead of
silently stacking independent policies. The appropriate layer depends on which
work would be repeated and which failure information it has.

### 3) **Read a circuit breaker as a stateful admission decision**

A **circuit breaker** monitors specified failure evidence for a protected operation
and can stop admitting calls to that dependency. In a common model:

| State | Meaning at this breaker |
|---|---|
| Closed | Protected calls are admitted and their outcomes contribute to the policy |
| Open | Calls through this breaker are rejected without invoking the protected dependency |
| Half-open | A limited trial policy admits calls to assess whether normal admission should resume |

The electrical naming can be surprising: open means calls are stopped. Fowler's
[original circuit-breaker explanation](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CircuitBreaker.html)
describes the state transitions and the need to handle rejected calls. Failure
thresholds, sampling windows, trial limits and transitions are implementation
policies, not facts established by those state names.

For a fictional email dependency, several qualifying failures cause the breaker
to open. The next request gets a local dependency-unavailable outcome without an
email call. After a configured interval, the breaker permits a trial. A successful
trial may return it to closed under the policy; a failed trial may reopen it.
Neither opening nor a successful probe repairs previously unsent confirmations.
Their recovery remains a separate product and delivery responsibility.

A breaker is not a retry loop, an idempotency mechanism or a guarantee that the
remote system is down. Its observation may concern one client, operation or
connection path. Incorrect thresholds can reject useful work, and trial behavior
needs testing. If the product permits delayed email, recording pending work may
be an option; presenting an unsent message as successfully delivered is not.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A timeout is evidence that the caller's waiting condition expired, not proof of
remote rollback. Similarly, retrying a timed-out request can duplicate an effect
unless the actual contract prevents it. Keep one business operation's identity
separate from the identities of its attempts.

Backoff reduces retry frequency; jitter spreads timing; a circuit breaker can
stop selected calls. These mechanisms address different decisions. More retries,
a longer timeout and a breaker are not interchangeable ways to claim reliability.

### 5) **Performance note**

Attempts consume dependency capacity, while waiting can retain resources. Short
limits can cause unnecessary retries; long limits can tie up callers. Backoff
and jitter can reduce bursts while increasing completion time for an individual
operation. A breaker trades attempted work for immediate rejection under its
policy. The arithmetic above counts a constructed budget and amplification only;
no service latency distribution, retry success rate, breaker recovery time or
load test has been measured.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner what is known at 100 ms and what new evidence changes at 120 ms.
Then give them the delay table and the remaining 750 ms budget. A sound answer
rejects the full schedule without pretending the operation failed before committing,
and distinguishes attempts, safe repetition, scheduling and breaker admission.
For a breaker, ask what happens to an earlier unsent confirmation after a later
probe succeeds; it still needs its own recovery evidence.
