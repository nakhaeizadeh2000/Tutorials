## 4.2. Backpressure buffering throttling and load shedding

A queue can absorb a burst, but it cannot make a permanently slower consumer
catch up with a permanently faster producer. To review an overload claim, identify
where work waits, where its source is slowed and where work is refused.

Read the queue, consumer and retry lessons. All rates and queue counts below are
a constructed accounting model, not a measured workload or capacity recommendation.

### 1) **Separate retained work from feedback that regulates its source**

A **buffer** temporarily holds data or work between stages that progress at
different rates. **Buffering** can absorb timing differences. A queue is one
possible organization for that waiting work. A **backlog** is accumulated pending
work; state whether a reported count includes only queued work or also items
already executing.

**Backpressure** communicates a downstream capacity or demand constraint upstream
so the producing side can regulate what it sends. A consumer might request only
a bounded number of records, or a producer might pause after a full-buffer signal.
The mechanism must actually connect the constrained stage to the producing stage.
Simply measuring queue length does not create backpressure.

The [Reactive Streams initiative](https://www.reactive-streams.org/) describes
non-blocking demand coordination across asynchronous boundaries to avoid forcing
arbitrary buffering on a receiver. That specific standard does not define every
application's network or business policy. Node's
[stream backpressure guide](https://nodejs.org/learn/modules/backpressuring-in-streams)
provides another concrete setting: a producer must respect the stream's capacity
signals. These examples illustrate a relationship; installing a library does not
prove that every stage of an application follows it.

A bounded consumer that immediately copies everything into an unbounded list
has only moved the backlog. Likewise, reading broker messages slowly does not
necessarily slow external clients submitting new bookings. Trace the full path
and identify the point at which the original source can pause, reduce its rate,
receive a rejection or persist work under another explicit contract.

### 2) **Distinguish throttling from rejecting excess work**

**Throttling** constrains work according to a rate, quota or concurrency policy.
Its implementation may delay, pace or reject work, so ask which behavior the word
means in this system. The earlier API lesson owns rate-limit scope and response
metadata; a client-side timer throttle is a different mechanism with a related goal.

**Load shedding** deliberately refuses or drops selected work to protect useful
service under resource pressure. **Admission control** decides which incoming work is accepted. It can reject a request before
expensive processing. The response and client behavior must make the consequence
clear; pretending a refused booking was accepted would change the product contract.

| Constructed mechanism | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Buffer up to 20 waiting jobs | Where they are retained, what happens at the limit and what success was promised |
| Pause production when the downstream stage signals no capacity | Which source receives the signal and whether it cooperates |
| Permit a bounded rate or concurrent count | Counting scope, release rule and delay-versus-rejection behavior |
| Refuse selected new work under overload | Selection policy, visible outcome and treatment of already-accepted work |

These categories can overlap. Rejecting requests above a capacity policy may be
both throttling and load shedding. A circuit breaker instead uses scoped failure
evidence to decide whether to call a dependency; its open state does not necessarily
mean the current server's own queue is full.

Google's [first-hand overload discussion](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)
explains client-side regulation, request-cost differences and criticality policies.
Its team-specific priority names and numerical tuning are not universal rules.
A service should decide which work can be delayed, degraded or refused without
silently abandoning its accepted obligations.

### 3) **Account for a growing backlog and change one policy**

The fictional report worker starts with no pending jobs. In each one-second
window, exactly 12 jobs enter and 8 finish. Treat jobs as equal-cost units and
assume arrivals and completions are smoothly interleaved so pending work grows
steadily by four jobs per second. Account at window boundaries and ignore failures,
retries, separate in-flight counts and other stages. The waiting buffer can retain at most 20 jobs. The first rows assume
all arrivals are admitted while the boundary count remains within that limit.

| End of window | Total arrivals | Total completions | Pending jobs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12 | 8 | 4 |
| 2 | 24 | 16 | 8 |
| 3 | 36 | 24 | 12 |
| 4 | 48 | 32 | 16 |
| 5 | 60 | 40 | 20 |

The sixth unchanged window would require space for 24 pending jobs. A policy must
now affect admission, production, retention or service capacity. The exact
within-window queue peak is outside this simplified model; the table is boundary
accounting, not a discrete-event simulation of scheduling.

Compare these explicit variations starting from the 20 pending jobs:

| Next-window policy | Result under the model |
|---|---|
| Source cooperates and sends only 6 jobs; 8 finish | Pending count falls to 18 |
| 12 arrive, 4 are explicitly refused, 8 finish | 8 are admitted and pending count stays 20; four callers receive refusal |
| Buffer limit increases to 40; 12 enter, 8 finish | Pending count becomes 24; the rate mismatch remains |
| Processing capacity actually rises to 14; 12 enter, 14 finish | Pending count falls to 18 while enough work is available |

The first is a successful feedback response only if a real mechanism caused the
source to regulate itself. The second makes a user-visible admission decision.
The third buys space and time without increasing completed work. The fourth
requires evidence that resources and dependencies really support the higher
completion rate; adding a worker process alone does not establish it.

Practice: arrivals stop after window five. At 8 completions per second, the
remaining 20 jobs require 2.5 seconds of aggregate processing in this idealized
constant-rate model. A real system with variable job sizes, scheduling, storage
waits or batching can differ. This is a drain estimate, not a promised completion
deadline for every individual job.

### 4) **Common confusion**

Backpressure is feedback that affects upstream production, not another name for
a queue or for dropping data. A bounded buffer still needs a full-buffer policy.
If a producer ignores the signal, the system needs another explicit way to handle
excess work rather than claiming an end-to-end bound.

Throttling also does not imply that every delayed request will eventually succeed.
Deadlines can expire, clients can disconnect and policies can reject work. If
clients immediately retry every shed request, they can recreate overload. The
previous retry lesson explains why attempts need their own limits and timing.

### 5) **Performance note**

Buffers retain memory or storage and add waiting time. Larger buffers may absorb
bursts while increasing the age of pending work; stale work may have little value.
Request counts also hide varying CPU, memory and dependency costs. Measure the
actual constrained resource and completion boundary before selecting a policy.
The equal-cost model demonstrates conservation of admitted and completed jobs,
not throughput under load, tail latency, memory per job or a scalable worker count.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to point to the backlog after each stage, including any list
outside the official queue. Then ask what observable change proves that a claimed
backpressure mechanism reached the source. For the table, require them to account
for admitted, refused and completed jobs separately. A sound answer preserves
accepted work's obligations and avoids describing a larger buffer as more capacity
to finish work.
