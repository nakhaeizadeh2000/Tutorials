## 3.2. Streams batches consumers acknowledgments and dead-letter queues

A consumer can receive a message without applying its business change. It can
also apply the change before recording its progress. Words such as consumed,
acknowledged and committed need a boundary before they become useful evidence.

Read the preceding routing lesson. The packets below are constructed lifecycle
and progress records, not live Kafka or RabbitMQ observations.

### 1) **Separate a flow of records from a bounded processing group**

A **stream** in this messaging context is a sequence or flow of records made
available over time. **Stream processing** handles that flow incrementally rather
than requiring the whole future input to arrive first. A stream can end; the word
does not require an infinite source or a specific network protocol.

A **batch** is a bounded group processed together. A consumer can fetch a batch
from an ongoing stream, so stream and batch are not mutually exclusive system
labels. For example, a report updater might receive the next hundred available
booking records together, process that group, then request more. Grouping input
does not automatically make all its output changes one atomic transaction.

Some messaging systems retain a log that readers revisit by position. Kafka's
[4.3 introduction](https://kafka.apache.org/43/getting-started/introduction/)
describes partitioned topics and records retained independently of consumption.
This differs from a delivery queue that removes handled messages under its queue
contract. Neither label promises permanent history: inspect retention, compaction,
capacity, permissions and the recovery point actually available.

A Kafka **offset** identifies a position within one topic partition. A **consumer
group** is a logical subscriber whose member consumers divide partition assignments
in the KafkaConsumer model. Different groups can track separate progress through
the same retained records. Do not assume an offset is a timestamp, a global event
number or an identifier for a completed external business action.

### 2) **Read acknowledgments and checkpoints at their actual boundary**

An **acknowledgment**, often **ack**, signals an outcome under a particular protocol
or application contract. A negative acknowledgment, often **nack**, signals a
negative delivery outcome with handling determined by its flags and configuration.
It is not automatically a request to retry forever.

RabbitMQ distinguishes **publisher confirms**, from broker to publisher, from
**consumer delivery acknowledgments**, from consumer to broker. Its
[acknowledgment guide](https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/confirms) explicitly separates
the two. A publisher confirm does not report whether email was delivered. A consumer
ack allows the broker to consider the delivery handled; the consumer must decide
when its required processing has actually succeeded. Automatic acknowledgment
can move that boundary earlier than completion of the business work.

A **checkpoint** records progress for later continuation or recovery. In Kafka's
[consumer position reference](https://kafka.apache.org/43/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/consumer/KafkaConsumer.html),
the current position and committed position are distinct. Fetching records can
advance the current position before their application effects are complete.
A committed offset records where consumption should resume; it does not inspect
an external database and prove its writes succeeded.

Read this original single-partition packet. The application contract uses offsets
as the next input to read, records 40 and 41 are available, and no later records
are involved:

| Observation | Supported conclusion |
|---|---|
| Last committed offset is 40 | Recovery is recorded to resume at 40 under this contract |
| Fetch returns records 40 and 41; current position becomes 42 | Both records reached the consumer API |
| Output evidence confirms record 40's report change, but 41's change failed | This batch's required report changes are incomplete |
| An early commit stores offset 42 despite that failure | Progress was recorded past an unapplied required record |

Restarting from that committed position does not by itself revisit 41. The
retained record may support an explicit recovery procedure, but the early
checkpoint has not repaired the report. A useful handoff names the failed input,
the output evidence and the incorrectly advanced progress boundary.

Now remove the output-evidence row. The fetch and commit still do not prove
successful report processing, but you may no longer assert a known failure for
41. Alternatively, add verified output for 41 before committing 42. That resolves
this packet's missing output; it does not establish every crash/retry outcome.
Later delivery-semantics study examines that wider coordination problem.

### 3) **Treat failed-message isolation as the start of recovery**

A **dead-letter queue**, or **DLQ**, is a destination for messages diverted from
normal handling under a failure or lifecycle policy. It may actually be implemented
as a queue or topic. A **poison message** is a message that repeatedly fails the
consumer's processing path, for example because the expected schema and payload
disagree. The cause may be a consumer defect or configuration mismatch; the label
does not prove that the producer is at fault.

RabbitMQ's [dead-letter guide](https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/dlx) describes a
**dead-letter exchange**, or **DLX**, that routes dead-lettered messages to queues.
Rejection without requeue, expiration and configured delivery limits are among
the triggers. The DLX is a routing component, not the DLQ itself. Dead-letter
republishing can also fail, so a policy declaration is weaker evidence than an
observed retained message at the intended destination.

Consider a separate constructed packet. Message m17 reaches an email consumer.
Its payload has a string seat count where this consumer requires an integer.
Validation fails before an email is sent. The configured rejection route sends
the message to a DLQ, whose retained m17 entry and failure reason are then inspected.
The supported statement is: “m17 is isolated for investigation; its email action
has not completed.” It is not “the error is fixed because the main queue is clear.”

Assign an owner to investigate, preserve the original identity and useful error
context, and determine the correction and replay scope. A corrected consumer may
be needed instead of changing valid producer data. Replaying unchanged input into
the same rejecting path can simply repeat the failure. Verify the intended output
and progress record after recovery, while accounting for any external action that
may already have happened. Avoid indiscriminately copying sensitive message bodies
into broadly visible logs.

Confluent's [original error-handling walkthrough](https://www.confluent.io/blog/kafka-connect-deep-dive-error-handling-dead-letter-queues/)
contrasts failed conversion, silent skipping and retained error context. Its
historical connector commands are not required here; the transferable question
is whether continued processing still exposes the missing business output.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A successful receive, successful parse, completed business effect and recorded
checkpoint are separate observations. Calling all of them consumed hides the
boundary that matters after failure. Likewise, a broker's publisher confirmation
is not the consumer's result.

A DLQ need not contain only malformed data. Expired or repeatedly unsuccessful
deliveries can reach it under policy. An empty main queue or a running consumer
therefore does not establish that every required record produced its result.

### 5) **Performance note**

Batches can amortize per-request and coordination costs, while requiring memory
and potentially waiting to collect a group. Larger groups can increase the amount
of work repeated or reconciled after a failure, depending on checkpoint policy.
Stream retention uses storage and makes recovery depend on available history.
A DLQ can let other work continue while leaving unresolved output behind. This
lesson measures none of those costs; choose batch, progress and failure boundaries
before comparing throughput or report freshness.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to draw four boxes: received, validated, effect completed and
progress recorded. Place each packet row in its box. Then remove the known-failure
row and require a less certain diagnosis. For m17, ask which evidence establishes
isolation and which new evidence would establish recovery. A sound answer does
not treat transport progress or diverted input as completed business work.
