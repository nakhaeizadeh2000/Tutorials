## 1.2. Pull request, review, and conflicts

### 1) **Pull request proposes, review disposes**

**Definitions** — A *pull request* (PR; *merge request* on GitLab) proposes merging a branch — the change *plus its conversation*: description, diff, checks, comments, approvals. A *review* is the team's examination; *approval* accepts responsibility for the change landing, not merely politeness.

- **What problem it solves**: unreviewed merges make every author the single point of failure for their own blind spots. The PR ritual distributes knowledge (two people now understand the change) and catches the bugs the author's brain autocorrected — which is most of them.
- **Modern guidance** — keep PRs small (under ~400 lines changed — review effectiveness collapses past that), describe *why* in the body, and link the ticket. A PR that says "fixes AUTH-123: session expiry now absolute per spec §4" gets reviewed today; one that says "auth fixes" gets reviewed never.

```text
# Bad: conversation-free proposal (reviewers must excavate intent)
Title: "fixes" — Body: (empty) — 1,400 lines changed.

# Good: proposal with its conversation attached
Title: "fix(auth): absolute session expiry per AUTH-123"
Body: "Why: sliding expiry kept admin sessions alive indefinitely.
 What: expire at created+8h; migration backfills existing rows.
 Risk: forces re-login once. Tests: unit + e2e on expiry path."
```

### 2) **Conflicts are overlapping edits, not errors**

A *merge conflict* means two branches edited the same lines — the tool stops and asks a human to decide the union. It is a routine collaboration event, not breakage, and its frequency measures how long branches live apart.

- **Why it matters (flow, not fear)**: juniors dread conflicts as failures; seniors read them as information — "someone else is in this code, talk to them". Short-lived branches synced daily produce trivial conflicts; month-old branches produce archaeological ones. The fix for painful conflicts is process (integrate often), not cleverness.
- **Modern guidance** — resolve deliberately, never by accepting "both" blindly: read both intents, produce the union that preserves each, run the affected tests, and commit the resolution separately so it reviews cleanly. Then ask why the overlap happened — repeated conflicts in one file signal missing ownership boundaries.

### 3) **Review words that move work**

*LGTM* (looks good to me) approves; *nit* (nitpick) flags trivia the author may take or leave; *blocking* vs *non-blocking* marks whether the comment must resolve before merge. *Request changes* formally withholds approval; *dismiss* (a stale approval after new commits) re-arms the gate.

- **Why it matters (correctness, velocity)**: vague review language stalls work — "maybe change this?" leaves the author guessing whether merge is allowed. Explicit words move queues: blocking = stop, non-blocking/nit = proceed at will, LGTM = go. Teams that label every comment ship faster with fewer re-reviews.
- **Modern guidance** — prefix or tag every comment's force: "blocking: …", "nit: …", "question (non-blocking): …". Approve with conditions stated ("LGTM after the timeout nit is addressed — no re-review needed") so authors merge without another round-trip. Silence is not approval — the button is.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "Small PRs mean slow progress" — small PRs mean *fast* progress: reviewed in hours, revertable cleanly, bisectable. The 2,000-line PR is the slow one — it waits a week and then breaks the bisect.
- "Conflicts mean someone did something wrong" — they mean two people worked in parallel, which is the entire point of branches.
- "Approval means the code is perfect" — approval means "acceptable risk, understandable, tested enough" — a judgment call with an owner, not a correctness proof.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Coach both sides of the table: authors learn proposal-craft (small diffs, why-led descriptions); reviewers learn comment-force labeling and 24-hour turnaround. Review turnaround time is the metric that predicts team velocity better than any other — measure it, publish it, and treat slow reviews as the bottleneck they are.
