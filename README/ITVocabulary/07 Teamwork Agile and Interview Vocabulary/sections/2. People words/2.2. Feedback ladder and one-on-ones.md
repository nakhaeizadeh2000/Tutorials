## 2.2. Feedback, ladder, and one-on-ones

### 1) **Feedback is specific and soon**

**Definition** — Useful *feedback* names a behavior, its impact, and a request: "in yesterday's review you approved without running tests (behavior), and main broke for two hours (impact) — please verify green before LGTM (request)". *Radical candor* (care + directness) is the culture where this flows both directions, including upward.

- **What problem it solves**: vague feedback ("be more careful", "good job") changes nothing — the receiver cannot map it to any action. Specific-soon feedback compounds: small corrections weekly prevent the dossier-style reckoning where six months of silence detonate in a performance review nobody saw coming.
- **Modern guidance** — give it within days (fresh context, low stakes), ask for it explicitly ("what one thing should I change?"), and receive it with notes-not-defense (write it down, ask clarifying questions, decide later). Feedback independence — seeking it before it's forced — is the fastest documented career accelerator.

```text
# Bad: vague and late (unactionable, then explosive)
[6 months silence] "Your code quality needs improvement."
 [No example, no path, maximum anxiety.]

# Good: specific and soon (behavior → impact → request)
"Tuesday's PR merged with red CI (behavior) — blocked
 the team till noon (impact). Request: re-run checks
 after last push before merging. Agreed?"
```

### 2) **Ladder calibrates growth**

A career *ladder* (levels, leveling matrix) defines scope and expectations per level — junior (guided tasks) → mid (independent delivery) → senior (ambiguous problems, multiplies others) → staff+ (org-level leverage). *Promotion* is scope-demonstrated-then-recognized, not tenure-awarded.

- **Why it matters (fairness, motivation)**: unlabeled growth runs on vibes — promotions feel like lottery, feedback feels arbitrary, and good engineers leave for places that can articulate their worth. Ladders convert "am I senior yet?" into checkable criteria (scope of ambiguity handled, others multiplied, systems owned).
- **Modern guidance** — read your ladder twice a year with your manager, collect evidence per criterion continuously (brag document — a running log of impact, written weekly while memory is fresh), and target the *scope gap* explicitly ("I deliver independently; next: lead an ambiguous migration end-to-end"). Promotions follow demonstrated scope — manufacture the demonstrations deliberately.

### 3) **One-on-ones are yours, not theirs**

*One-on-ones* (1:1s) are the report's meeting with their manager — for career, blockers, context, and early warnings — with the agenda set from below. Status updates are the *misuse*; growth and obstacles are the use.

- **Why it matters (retention, trajectory)**: canceled 1:1s are the canary of disengagement — first the meeting slips, then the feedback stops, then the resignation letter cites "growth". Protected, report-driven 1:1s surface blockers while they're small and careers while they're steerable.
- **Modern guidance** — bring the agenda (top blockers, one career topic, feedback for your manager — upward feedback keeps the relationship honest), never accept recurring cancellation (reschedule same-week, always), and track outcomes (decisions and promises in writing — memory is not a system). Managers with ten reports forget verbal agreements; written ones survive.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "Feedback means criticism" — recognition is feedback too ("your incident write-up set the team standard — do more of that"); direction-only feedback cultures starve people of signal about what to repeat.
- "Ladder-climbing is political" — visibility of impact is not politics; politics is credit-taking — documenting real work and seeking scope is the honest game the ladder rewards.
- "1:1s are for managers to check on me" — inverted: they are the report's leverage over management attention — the highest-ROI thirty minutes in your calendar, if you drive them.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Start every 1:1 you run with the report's agenda, end with one growth question ("what did you learn this week? what's one thing I can unblock?"). Juniors who experience good 1:1s replicate them when they lead — the format transmits culturally, one well-run meeting at a time, which is why your 1:1 quality is a legacy decision, not a calendar entry.
