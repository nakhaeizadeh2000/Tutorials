## 2.1. Roles, stakeholders, and ownership

### 1) **Roles name accountabilities**

**Definitions** — *PM* (product manager) owns *what and why* (priorities, requirements); *designer* owns user experience; *engineer* owns *how* (implementation, quality); *EM* (engineering manager) owns team health and delivery capability. *TPM* (technical program manager) coordinates across teams when scope exceeds one.

- **What problem it solves**: unnamed accountabilities default to either duplication (three people deciding the API shape) or vacuum (nobody deciding it). Explicit roles convert "someone should" into a name — and disagreements into negotiations between named owners instead of hallway campaigns.
- **Modern guidance** — ask "who decides X?" for every recurring decision (scope, design, dates, quality bar) and write the answers down. Healthy overlap is fine (engineers questioning requirements, PMs questioning estimates); healthy means *questioning*, not *deciding* — decision rights stay with the role.

```text
# Bad: accountability vacuum (decisions by loudest voice)
"API shape undecided for 2 weeks — PM assumed engineering,
 engineering assumed PM, designer mocked both versions."

# Good: named accountabilities (disagreement with an owner)
"API shape: engineer proposes (Mina), PM approves fit (Jonas),
 designer reviews UX surface (Sara). Decision Friday by Jonas."
```

### 2) **Stakeholder means affected-and-vocal**

A *stakeholder* is anyone with skin in the outcome — users, support, sales, legal, leadership — whether they attend your meetings or ambush your launch. *Stakeholder management* means mapping them early, informing proportionally, and collecting constraints before they become blockers.

- **Why it matters (launches, careers)**: projects rarely die from technical failure; they die from unmapped stakeholders ("legal never approved that data use", "support can't staff this flow", "sales promised the opposite"). Each surprise costs multiples of the early conversation that would have prevented it.
- **Modern guidance** — map before building: list affected parties, note each one's veto power and core concern, set a communication cadence per tier (demo invites for deciders, notes for the informed). "Who else cares about this?" asked in week one is the cheapest risk management in the industry.

### 3) **Ownership is DRI, not tenancy**

*DRI* (directly responsible individual) means one neck per problem — a named human who ensures resolution (by doing, delegating, or escalating), even in shared code nobody "owns". *Tenancy* (my service, my files) without DRI produces well-kept silos and ownerless gaps between them.

- **Why it matters (incidents, velocity)**: ownerless problems wait — each team assumes another covers the shared library, the flaky pipeline, the aging docs. DRI converts waiting into motion: the DRI need not fix it personally, but must guarantee it moves (delegate with a date, escalate with context).
- **Modern guidance** — assign DRIs to problems, not just systems (migration X, flake-rate Y, docs-area Z), rotate thankless ownerships (build health, on-call-adjacent chores), and review the DRI list quarterly — stale ownership (departed, overloaded) is how gaps silently reopen.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "Flat teams need no roles" — flat removes hierarchy, not accountabilities; role-less teams decide by stamina, and stamina correlates with loudness, not correctness.
- "Stakeholder means manager" — the support agent fielding user anger is a stakeholder with sharper constraints than most executives; map by affectedness, not title.
- "Ownership means doing it yourself" — DRI means *ensuring* (delegate, escalate, track) — heroes do everything personally and become bottlenecks wearing capes.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Pair every junior with one ownable problem early (a flaky test suite, a docs corner, a small migration) as named DRI — with coaching guardrails, not abandonment. Owned problems teach the full loop (diagnose → plan → execute → report) that task-tickets never do; graduates of one DRI stint operate a level above their tenure.
