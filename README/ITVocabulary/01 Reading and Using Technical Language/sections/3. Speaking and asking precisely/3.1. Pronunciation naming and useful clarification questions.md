## 3.1. Pronunciation naming and useful clarification questions

You understand a term on the page but hesitate to say it in a meeting. Then
someone asks whether "the client" is ready, and you are unsure whether they mean
the customer or the application. These are different problems: how a name sounds
and what the speaker means. You can resolve either without pretending to know.

This is a spoken/written communication exercise. It needs no software or audio
equipment. The earlier lessons supply the distinction between an acronym's
expansion, a concept's meaning and the context selecting that meaning.

### 1) **Make names recognizable without treating pronunciation as a skill test**

An unfamiliar written name may be pronounced as a word, letter by letter or
according to a project's convention. Start with a recognizable pronunciation
you know. If recognition fails, spell the name or show its written form. Ask
"How do you usually say that name?" when learning the local convention.

For example, API is commonly spoken "A-P-I." RAM is commonly spoken as a word.
SQL is heard both as letter names and as "sequel." The spelling can identify
the same term despite the spoken variation. Pronunciation is therefore a useful
communication detail, not proof of whether someone understands the technology.

In English, choose **a** or **an** according to the initial sound you actually
use. "An API" starts with the vowel sound in the spoken letter A. "A URL" fits
the common "you-are-ell" pronunciation because the first sound is consonantal.
Different pronunciations can change the appropriate article. Google's
[abbreviation guide](https://developers.google.com/style/abbreviations) treats
pronunciation and audience convention as relevant, rather than relying on the
first written letter alone.

Do not make an entire discussion wait for a pronunciation dispute. Showing the
exact name usually resolves recognition faster. A team can agree a spoken
convention while accepting a newcomer's understandable variation.

### 2) **Preserve exact names when the task depends on identity**

A spoken nickname can conceal several distinct objects. Suppose teammates say
"orders test," while two installations are named `OrdersTest` and `OrdersTest2`.
Asking for "the test one" still leaves two candidates. Put the actual name in
the message and ask which one is involved.

When quoting a setting, identifier, file name or error message, preserve its
spelling and punctuation. Whether case differences change behavior depends on
the relevant system; do not silently normalize them. In this constructed
example, `waitSeconds` identifies a setting, while "waiting time" describes its
purpose. The prose description helps understanding; the exact identifier helps
the reader find the right entry in the reference.

This does not mean copying sensitive values into a conversation. A useful
question can name the setting and version while omitting a secret credential.
Identity of the option and disclosure of its private value are different needs.

### 3) **Ask for the smallest fact that moves the discussion forward**

A useful question tells the other person where your understanding stops. Compare:

> Explain the system.

with this constructed clarification:

> I understand that FieldNotes is the application making the request. By
> client, do you mean FieldNotes here, or the customer using it?

The second question shares a tentative interpretation and makes the ambiguity
easy to correct. Once the actor is clear, ask the next relevant question. Do not
bundle twenty unrelated unknowns into one message when one missing distinction
blocks the rest.

Julia Evans describes asking specific factual questions and stating a current
understanding in her original account of
[getting useful answers](https://jvns.ca/blog/2021/10/21/how-to-get-useful-answers-to-your-questions/).
These are tactics to help a conversation, not prerequisites someone must satisfy
before deserving help. An honest "I do not know what that word means" is a
valid starting point.

Different gaps need different questions:

| Gap | Useful question |
|---|---|
| Meaning | What does rollout mean in this instruction? |
| Identity | Do you mean the installation named OrdersTest or OrdersTest2? |
| Relationship | Is FieldNotes making the request, or receiving it? |
| Evidence | Which result showed that the request failed? |
| Scope | Does this statement describe version 2.4, or the current release? |

After an answer, summarize what changed in your understanding. For example,
"So client means FieldNotes in this report; I still need the observed result."
This makes a remaining gap visible rather than closing the discussion with a
polite but inaccurate "understood."

Practice: someone says "Use the stable client in test." Write a question without
claiming the instruction is wrong. One answer is "Which client name/version and
which test installation should I use?" If stable refers to a publisher's status,
ask where that status is documented. Avoid inventing an approved version or
assuming every test installation has the same configuration.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A short question is not necessarily precise. "Which one?" can work when the
shared context is obvious; across an asynchronous discussion, naming the two
candidates helps preserve that context. Conversely, a long message can remain
vague if it never identifies the decision or missing fact.

Asking a yes/no question also does not mean forcing a false binary. If neither
candidate fits, invite a correction: "Or is client referring to another program?"
The goal is a correct shared model, not making the answer match your guesses.

### 5) **Performance note**

Pronunciation and clarification do not directly change program performance.
They can affect the quality of a performance investigation by keeping people
focused on the same installation, operation and result. Asking for the right
identifier prevents comparing unrelated observations; it does not substitute
for collecting those observations or measuring their cost.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Role-play a conversation with one ambiguous name and one unfamiliar term.
Have the learner ask a focused question, then summarize the answer with the
remaining uncertainty. As the answerer, correct misunderstandings plainly and
welcome follow-ups. Evaluate whether the participants now mean the same thing;
do not grade accent or reward confident guessing.
