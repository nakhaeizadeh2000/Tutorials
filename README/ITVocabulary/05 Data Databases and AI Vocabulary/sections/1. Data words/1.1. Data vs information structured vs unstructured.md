## 1.1. Data vs information, structured vs unstructured

### 1) **Data is raw, information is answered**

**Definition** — *Data* is recorded fact without interpretation (log lines, sensor readings, click events). *Information* is data processed into answers (the dashboard, the report, the alert) — processing adds the meaning, and meaning is what decisions consume.

- **What problem it solves**: teams hoard data and starve for information — petabytes logged, no dashboard answering "are checkouts failing more than yesterday?" The vocabulary forces the question early: who consumes this, and what decision does it change? Data with no consumer is cost without value (storage bills, PII liability, zero insight).
- **Modern guidance** — name the consumer and the decision before collecting: "we log checkout attempts (data) so support sees failure reasons per hour (information) and pages payments on spikes (decision)". Collection without that sentence is hoarding — delete it or justify it.

```text
# Bad: hoarding-talk (data with no consumer)
"Log everything — storage is cheap, we might need it."

# Good: consumer-first (data → information → decision)
"Log checkout attempts with reason codes → hourly failure
 dashboard for support → page payments if card-declined
 share doubles. Storage cost: ~2 GB/day. PII: none."
```

### 2) **Structured vs unstructured**

*Structured* data fits rows and schemas (tables, CSVs, JSON with fixed fields) — queryable, countable, joinable. *Unstructured* data doesn't (free text, images, audio, video) — rich, but needing extraction (parsing, transcription, embeddings) before it answers questions.

- **Why it matters (tooling, cost)**: the shape decides the store and the bill — structured lives in databases and answers SQL in milliseconds; unstructured lives in object stores and lakes, answered by search indexes or models at orders-of-magnitude higher cost. Mismatching shape to tool ("full-text search over a relational table of PDFs") is how queries go from milliseconds to minutes.
- **Modern guidance** — structure at write time what you will question at read time (timestamps, IDs, status codes as real columns — not buried in message strings); keep the raw unstructured original alongside for reprocessing. The schema is a bet on future questions — bet explicitly, revisit yearly.

### 3) **Dataset, sample, and label in one line each**

A *dataset* is a collection gathered for a purpose (training, analysis, benchmarking). A *sample* (row, record, example) is one member. A *label* is the attached answer-key (this image = "cat", this transaction = "fraud") — what supervised learning learns to predict.

- **What problem it solves**: ML discussions collapse without these three — "the data is bad" could mean too few samples, wrong labels, or a dataset that doesn't represent production. Naming which one aims the fix: collect more, relabel carefully, or resample to match reality.
- **Modern guidance** — quote datasets with three facts (size, source, freshness) and labels with provenance (who labeled, agreement rate). "10k support tickets labeled by two agents, 91% agreement, refreshed monthly" is a dataset description; "some tickets" is a rumor.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "Big data means valuable data" — volume without consumers is liability (breach surface, compliance scope); value lives in answered questions, not bytes.
- "Unstructured means disorganized" — it means *unschemaed*; well-managed lakes with catalogs and lineage are highly organized, just not tabular.
- "More labels always help" — noisy labels teach noise; label quality (agreement, guidelines, edge-case review) dominates label quantity past modest scale.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Force the data→information→decision sentence in every design review touching collection or logging. Juniors who practice it stop proposing "log everything" within weeks — and start deleting unused telemetry, which is the cheapest performance and compliance win most teams never claim.
