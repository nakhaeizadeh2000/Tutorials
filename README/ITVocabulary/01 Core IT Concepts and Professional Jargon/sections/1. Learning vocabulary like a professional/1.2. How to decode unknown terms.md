## 1.2. How to decode unknown terms

### 1) **Decompose before you search**

**Definition** — Most technical terms are compounds: split them into parts, guess each part's job, then verify against a source. "Micro-service" = small + service; "de-ploy" = remove from folded state (historically, unfolding a deployment); "re-try with back-off" = attempt again with growing waits.

- **What problem it solves**: searching a bare acronym ("what is SOW?") returns ten industries' worth of noise. Decomposing first gives you a hypothesis — "statement of... work?" — which turns a blind search into a confirmation check and makes the answer stick.
- **Modern guidance** — apply the three-part split mechanically: *prefix* (micro-, de-, re-, auto-), *root* (service, deploy, try), *suffix/context* (-tion, -er, the team that says it). Latin/Greek roots dominate computing (tele-, remote; -ware, goods), so a small root list decodes a surprising share of new terms.

```text
# Good: hypothesis first, then one targeted verification
Unknown term in a ticket: "idempotency key".
Split: idem (same) + potency (power/effect) → "same-effect".
Hypothesis: "a key that makes repeats have the same effect".
Verify: one docs search confirms — retries with the same key
do not double-charge. Hypothesis kept, term owned.
```

### 2) **Ask for the referent, not the definition**

In a new codebase, "what does it mean?" invites a lecture; "what does it point to in our system?" invites a file path, a dashboard, or a table name — which is what you actually need.

- **Why it works**: workplace terms are half global vocabulary, half local dialect. "The pipeline" means CI/CD everywhere but points to *your team's* Jenkins/GitHub Actions config specifically. Definitions give you the global half; referents give you the local half, which is the half that unblocks you.
- **Modern guidance** — ask in this shape: "when you say *X*, which file/service/dashboard do you mean here?" Seniors answer referent-questions in seconds and respect them; definition-questions cost them a paragraph and teach you less.

### 3) **Build a personal glossary habit**

Write each new term in your own words within 24 hours, with one example from your own work — or it evaporates by next week.

- **Why it hurts to skip (maintainability of your own knowledge)**: re-learning the same term five times costs more than writing it down once. Recognition ("I've seen this") without recall ("I can explain it") fails exactly when it matters — in interviews and incidents.
- **Modern guidance** — one line per term, three columns: *term → my words → where I met it*. Review the list weekly for a month; anything still fuzzy after that gets a full leaf-style write-up. Spaced repetition beats re-reading, and your glossary becomes interview prep for free.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "I should memorize the whole glossary first" — vocabulary learned without referents is trivia; learn terms at the point of contact with real systems.
- "Asking what words mean looks junior" — asking precisely ("which service do you mean by *the queue* here?") looks competent at every level; pretending you know looks junior.
- "AI will define everything for me" — definitions without referents still leave you lost in *your* codebase; use tools for the global half, humans for the local half.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Teach juniors the decompose-first reflex by refusing to answer bare "what does X mean?" questions directly: answer with "what do the parts suggest?" first, then confirm. Two weeks of this and they stop asking you about words entirely — they start asking you about systems, which is the conversation you actually want.
