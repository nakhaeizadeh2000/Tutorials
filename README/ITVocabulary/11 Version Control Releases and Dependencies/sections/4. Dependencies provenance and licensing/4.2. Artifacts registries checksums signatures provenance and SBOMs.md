## 4.2. Artifacts registries checksums signatures provenance and SBOMs

A download page says “signed, with provenance and an SBOM.” These are useful
forms of evidence, but they answer different questions. Before drawing a conclusion,
identify the exact downloaded artifact, who made each statement, and which checks
were actually performed.

Read the previous dependency-record lesson and the earlier checksum lesson first.
This page interprets original, constructed evidence cards. No download, signature
verification, vulnerability scan or build was executed for the cards.

### 1) **Connect a distribution name to the actual artifact**

An **artifact** is a produced work product. Here it means the packaged bytes a
consumer obtains, such as an SDK archive. A source repository and a built archive
are different artifacts: the repository URL alone does not identify every input
or transformation that produced the download. The earlier toolchain lesson owns
the broader producer/consumer distinction.

A **registry** supplies package metadata or content under its ecosystem's rules.
A **mirror** serves copies from another source; a **cache** reuses previously
obtained content. A registry endpoint can distribute an artifact that someone else
built. “Served by our registry” and “built by our builder” therefore need separate
evidence. A configured endpoint also does not prove which endpoint served a
particular retrieval; request or cache records may be needed.

A **checksum** or cryptographic **digest** compares specified bytes under a named
algorithm. First agree whether the input is an archive, extracted file or some
other representation. A match with a trustworthy expected digest supports the
byte comparison. If the only expected digest came from the same untrusted source
as a substituted archive, matching them does not establish the intended publisher.
The earlier checksum lesson demonstrates the representation distinction; do not
repeat its compression experiment to answer a publisher-identity question.

Chainguard's [dependency-control walkthrough](https://main.chainguard.dev.chainguard.app/supply-chain-security-101/npm-supply-chain-attacks)
usefully separates selection, retrieval, artifact evidence and execution. Treat
its product promotion separately from those diagnostic questions. This lesson
uses a fictional archive rather than relying on its incident claims.

### 2) **Separate a verified signature from an accepted build claim**

A **digital signature** binds signed data to a signing key through a cryptographic
verification operation. Its useful interpretation depends on which key or verified
identity you accept, what data was signed and the applicable trust rules. A valid
signature from an unexpected signer is not the same as acceptance by your policy.
A signed statement can also contain an inaccurate or unwanted claim.

For a concrete identity-based system, Sigstore's
[verification guidance](https://docs.sigstore.dev/quickstart/verification-cheat-sheet/)
requires the expected certificate identity and identity issuer. The identity can
refer to an automated workflow, rather than a person's email. Those expectations
must come from your intended project's trusted instructions or policy; copying
whatever identity appears in an unknown download defeats the comparison. Full
key management and identity protocols remain deeper security study.

An **attestation** is a statement about a subject, such as a particular artifact.
A signed attestation adds an authenticated statement under its verification and
trust conditions; it does not make every statement true by definition.
**Build provenance** records origin and production information, such as source,
builder and build inputs or parameters. The **builder** is the system responsible
for performing the build and producing the associated evidence.

SLSA means **Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts**. Its approved version
1.2 distinguishes supply-chain requirements and verification. The
[artifact-verification guide](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/verifying-artifacts)
compares the artifact's digest with the attestation subject, verifies the statement,
and checks expected source, builder and build parameters. The trust placed in
the build platform remains an assumption. “There is a provenance file” skips
these comparisons; “some provenance verified” still leaves its policy unspecified.

npm's [provenance limitations](https://docs.npmjs.com/generating-provenance-statements/#provenance-limitations)
make the outcome boundary explicit: provenance does not guarantee an absence of
malicious code. An unwanted program can have an accurately recorded origin.
Origin evidence and a review of the program's behavior serve different purposes.

### 3) **Read a component inventory and apply each evidence boundary**

An **SBOM**, or **software bill of materials**, is a structured inventory describing
software components and, where recorded, their relationships. It can include
names, versions, identifiers and license information. CycloneDX's
[SBOM introduction](https://cyclonedx.org/capabilities/sbom/) describes this component
view. CycloneDX and SPDX are names you may encounter for machine-readable SBOM
formats; the presence of either name does not establish a complete inventory.

Ask what the inventory covers: source dependencies, a build output, an installed
system, or another defined subject. Record its producer, generation time, method
and known omissions. An SBOM made before packaging may not fully describe a later
archive. A current, relevant inventory helps locate a component when a new advisory
appears; it is not itself an advisory evaluation or proof that a reported component
is exploitable. SLSA's [SBOM comparison](https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.2/faq#q-how-does-slsa-and-slsa-provenance-relate-to-sbom)
explains why component inventories and build provenance have different granularity.

Read this fictional release packet. `H-A` and `H-B` stand for two different complete
SHA-256 digests, not real digest strings. `C7` is a source-revision label. None is
an executable verification input.

| Record | Stated observation or rule |
|---|---|
| Downloaded SDK archive | Digest calculated as `H-A` |
| Team's prior acceptance policy | Expected builder `Build-A`, source `Reports`, revision `C7` |
| Provenance verification report | Signature verified under the team's configured trust; subject `H-A`; builder `Build-A`; source `Reports`; revision `C7`; expected build parameters matched |
| SBOM verification report | Signed inventory is valid under its configured trust; subject is `H-B`; it lists formatter version 1.7.2 |
| Application behavior check | Not performed for this release |

The provenance report supports the stated origin checks for the `H-A` archive
under that report's trust and verification assumptions. The SBOM report concerns
`H-B`. Its signature can be valid while its inventory remains inapplicable to
`H-A`; filenames or matching release labels cannot repair that subject mismatch.
The packet does not establish whether formatter 1.7.2 is in the downloaded archive.
Request an inventory bound to `H-A` and assess its scope and generation evidence.

The missing application check also remains missing. Even a matching SBOM would
not show that this SDK handles the application's inputs correctly. A useful review
states each result separately instead of reducing the packet to “secure.”

Practice: change only the provenance builder to `Build-B`, keeping its signature
valid and subject `H-A`. Does it pass the team's stated origin policy? No: the
verified statement now identifies a builder outside the expected value. Investigate
that difference rather than changing the expectation merely to obtain a pass.
If the team deliberately accepts a different builder after assessment, document
that decision; it is a policy change, not a newly discovered cryptographic fact.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A lockfile and an SBOM can overlap in component information, but their purposes
and subjects differ. A resolution record is not automatically an inventory of
every file or component in the final product. Link the record to the relevant
artifact before using it to answer “what did we ship?”

“Verified” needs an object and a rule. Verifying a signature, matching an artifact
subject, accepting a builder and checking application behavior are separate acts.
A valid signature on an irrelevant SBOM illustrates why one green result cannot
stand in for the others. Absence of an SBOM does not itself establish malware;
it leaves inventory questions without that evidence.

### 5) **Performance note**

Hashing requires reading the relevant bytes; signature and attestation verification
also consume computation and may require retrieving evidence. Inventory generation
adds its own inspection work. The cost depends on artifact size, tool, cache,
network and verification policy. These records do not benchmark those costs or
change the application's algorithmic speed. Reusing a verification result needs
a clear artifact identity and applicable policy; a result for `H-B` cannot save
work by being silently treated as verification of `H-A`.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Cover the packet's digest column and ask what “the SBOM is valid” establishes.
Then reveal `H-B` and ask the learner to revise the claim. They should distinguish
statement validity from applicability to the download. Use the `Build-B` variation
to separate authenticated identity from accepted identity. A strong explanation
also leaves behavioral safety and missing checks unresolved without inventing
an incident or prescribing an arbitrary signature policy.
