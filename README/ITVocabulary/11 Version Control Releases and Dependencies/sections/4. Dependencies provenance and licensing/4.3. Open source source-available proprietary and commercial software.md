## 4.3. Open source source-available proprietary and commercial software

“We cannot use it because it is commercial” and “we can copy it because the source
is public” both skip the question that matters: what rights apply to this exact
software for the intended activity? Price, source access and permission are
separate properties.

This lesson teaches classification and evidence questions, not a legal decision
about a particular product. Sources were checked on **2026-10-19**. The worked
records are fictional; no dependency or repository license is changed here.

### 1) **Separate source access and license rights from price**

A **software license** states permissions and conditions for covered software.
Source visibility describes what someone can inspect; it does not by itself grant
permission to modify or redistribute the work. Identify the covered version and
files as well as the terms: a repository can contain components with different
licenses.

**Open source software**, or **OSS**, grants rights that go beyond inspection.
The Open Source Initiative's [Open Source Definition](https://opensource.org/osd)
includes source access, redistribution and derived works, and prohibits exclusion
of business use. Those criteria distinguish an open source license from a label
that merely says the code is visible. Conditions can still apply; open source
is not a synonym for “no obligations.”

**Source-available** commonly describes software whose source can be inspected
but whose terms do not provide the full open source rights. A restriction against
commercial use is a clear example of a restriction incompatible with the Open
Source Definition. The label is not a standardized list of permissions; inspect
the actual terms rather than assuming all source-available products are alike.

**Proprietary software** commonly means software retained under terms that do not
grant the relevant open source or software-freedom rights. Its source may be closed
or available to selected people under restrictive conditions. Thus proprietary
and source-visible are not mutually exclusive descriptions. State the rights
being discussed instead of using “closed” to answer every permission question.

**Commercial software** concerns business activity, such as selling a product or
support. It can be open source or proprietary. **Free of charge** concerns price.
**Free software**, in the software-freedom sense, concerns users' freedoms rather
than a zero price. You may also encounter **FOSS**, meaning free and open source
software. The two communities have different emphases; expanding the abbreviation
does not settle a license's details. The OSI's [commercial-use and terminology FAQ](https://opensource.org/faq)
explains these distinctions. “Freeware” commonly means a no-charge offering and
should not be treated as a grant of source-modification rights.

### 2) **Read license conditions without replacing them with a slogan**

**Permissive** is commonly used for open source licenses that allow covered code
to be incorporated into works distributed under other terms, subject to their
conditions. **Copyleft** describes requirements that preserve specified freedoms
and license terms for covered works when the relevant conditions apply. Both
can be open source. These are families of approaches, not complete licenses.

Terminology itself varies: OSI's FAQ uses “permissive” broadly and cautions against
using it as the opposite of copyleft. This page follows the common narrower usage
when comparing license families, as in the Linux Foundation's
[license guide](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/licensebestpractices). Always name
the actual license and version when a decision depends on its terms.

For a concrete reading example, the [MIT License text](https://choosealicense.com/licenses/mit/)
grants broad rights, including modification, distribution and sale, with a
condition to retain its copyright and permission notices in copies or substantial
portions. It also contains warranty and liability disclaimers. “MIT means I can
remove all notices” therefore misreads the terms. This is a reading of that text,
not a finding that an arbitrary archive is entirely MIT-licensed or that its
publisher owns every included component.

Copyleft is likewise not “any use forces every file in a company to become public.”
The covered work, actual license, modification or combination, and distribution
or other applicable trigger matter. Different licenses set different boundaries.
License **compatibility** asks whether the requirements can be satisfied together
for a particular combination and use. It is distinct from API compatibility:
a program can execute successfully while its intended distribution needs further
license analysis.

A copyright notice identifies a claimed rights holder and related information;
it is not itself a license granting reuse rights. An **attribution** acknowledges
an author or source. Attribution can be required, but adding credit does not
substitute for permission where permission is needed. An SBOM's license field
helps locate information; it does not complete a review of the actual terms.

### 3) **Classify a release packet and identify the missing review**

Read these constructed records. The classifications concern the stated evidence,
not a real product or a jurisdiction-specific legal conclusion.

| Offering | Source and terms supplied | Price or business activity |
|---|---|---|
| Formatter A | Exact covered component supplied under unmodified MIT terms | Vendor charges for maintenance support |
| Viewer B | Source downloadable; custom terms prohibit commercial use | Download costs nothing |
| Editor C | Binary offered under a restrictive proprietary license; source not supplied | Download costs nothing |
| Helper D | Public repository; no reuse license established in the packet | No price listed |

Formatter A's paid support does not conflict with its open source licensing.
Viewer B is source-available under the described restriction; the zero price
does not make its terms open source. Editor C is a no-charge proprietary offering.
For Helper D, public access alone is insufficient evidence of open source reuse
rights. Do not fill an absent license field with a guess.

GitHub's [repository-licensing guide](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/licensing-a-repository)
separates a public repository's visibility and platform viewing/forking rights
from an open source license. Its license detection is an aid, not a replacement
for the terms. A repository being public is also different from a work being in
the **public domain**, a copyright-status concept rather than a visibility setting.

Suppose a team wants to modify Viewer B and distribute it inside a paid customer
product. The packet establishes a relevant use restriction, so “the download is
free” cannot justify that plan. Gather the exact license text and version, covered
components, intended modifications and distribution, then route the question to
the organization's license-review owner or qualified legal adviser. The owner
needs that concrete activity and those terms to assess it; “is this dependency
allowed?” is too vague.

Practice: change only Viewer B's price to a paid download. Does its license
classification change? No: the stated rights remain the same. Next, keep its
zero price but replace its custom terms with a verified open source license for
the covered component. The rights classification changes. The team's actual
combination and distribution still need their applicable conditions checked.

### 4) **Common confusion**

“Open source versus commercial” compares different dimensions. A company can sell
open source software or services around it. A proprietary product can cost nothing.
Ask separately about rights, source access and the offering's price or business
model; none supplies the other answers automatically.

“Permissive” does not mean “unlicensed,” and copyleft does not forbid business use.
Likewise, a top-level license badge does not establish the license of every copied
asset or bundled dependency. Preserve the exact terms and component boundaries
when explaining a result. Avoid treating another team's approval as universal
permission for a different use or a different version.

### 5) **Performance note**

A license label has no direct effect on the execution speed of identical code.
It can affect which component, service or distribution approach a team can choose,
which in turn can have operational costs. Those are separate decisions requiring
actual workload and terms. Open source does not imply lower latency, and a paid
license does not establish faster execution or included support. The packet
compares terminology; it contains no performance or total-cost measurement.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to describe each offering using three sentences: what source is
available, what rights the supplied terms establish, and what price or business
activity is stated. Then remove the terms column. They should lower their certainty
about rights rather than infer them from the remaining columns. For a real handoff,
ask them to name the version, covered files and intended action before requesting
review. Reward accurate uncertainty and useful evidence, not a memorized list of
“safe” and “unsafe” licenses.
