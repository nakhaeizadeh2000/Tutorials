## 2.1. Identification authentication authorization and accounting

A staff member signs in successfully but cannot download another team's roster.
That can be correct behavior: establishing identity evidence and granting access
are different decisions. “Auth failed” hides which question needs investigation.
Name the operation and the decision before proposing a password reset.

### 1) **Separate identifying a subject from verifying identity evidence**

**Identification** presents or selects an identity in a system's context. Entering
an account name says which account is claimed; it does not establish control of
that account. **Authentication**, commonly abbreviated **AuthN**, verifies evidence
supporting the claimed identity. A verifier might check proof of control of an
**authenticator**, a means used in that authentication process. The mechanism and
its assurance matter; merely copying a display name into a request is not that proof.

Digital identity is contextual. An account can represent a person in a particular
service without establishing their legal name or every real-world attribute.
**Identity proofing** evaluates evidence about a person's claimed identity during
the relevant enrollment process. It is distinct from a later authentication to
the account. Not every service requires real-name proofing, and a successful
sign-in is not automatically evidence that such proofing occurred.

The current [NIST Digital Identity Model](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63/model/)
in the SP 800-63-4 guidelines separates proofing, enrollment, accounts,
authenticators and verification. It describes verifier checks of possession and
control of authenticators bound to an active account. Those are scoped identity
roles, not proof that the person using an account has permission to perform every
service operation. Its human-subject model is also not a complete description of
all machine-identity arrangements.

**AAA** commonly expands to **Authentication, Authorization and Accounting** in
access-service discussions. Identification is a useful related distinction but
is not a fourth A in that expansion. The letters also name Arrange–Act–Assert in
testing; choose the context before interpreting them. “Auth” alone can mean
AuthN, AuthZ or a combined subsystem, so ask for the precise decision and result.

### 2) **Distinguish an access decision from its activity record**

**Authorization**, commonly **AuthZ**, decides whether an entity may perform a
particular action on a particular resource under the relevant policy and context.
Authentication evidence may be an input, but a successful identity check does not
grant every permission. A policy can also permit unauthenticated public access.
Authorization therefore does not universally mean “the step after login.”

OWASP's [authorization guide](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Authorization_Cheat_Sheet.html)
explicitly contrasts authenticated users with limited permissions and public
resources available without authentication. Keep the actor, action, resource and
policy visible. A page hiding its Download button is a client behavior; it does
not establish the server's access decision for a direct request to the export.

In AAA, **accounting** records relevant activity or resource use, such as session
start/end, an authorization decision, a transferred quantity or service consumption.
The intended use can include operational tracking or billing. It is not the same
as creating an account, and it is not restricted to financial accounting.
[RFC 2903](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2903.html) illustrates the historical
AAA architecture and accounting of authorization and service use. It is an
Experimental RFC from August 2000, not a current mandatory protocol or a universal
architecture for every application.

An **audit trail** is a record that can help reconstruct relevant actions and
decisions. **Auditing** examines evidence against stated criteria. Recording an
“allow” decision does not prove that the requested download completed; recording
a claimed account name alone does not establish that the requester authenticated
as that account. Records need appropriate identity, operation, resource, time and
outcome context for the question they are meant to answer. Their completeness,
protection and trustworthiness are separate matters, not guarantees from the
word “audit.”

These functions can share software or span several components. A successful check
at a gateway does not automatically establish the resource-level decision in the
service. Equally, the vocabulary does not require three distinct servers. Later
identity lessons introduce principals, credentials, tokens, sessions and protocol
roles; no particular login implementation is assumed here.

### 3) **Trace independent identity permission and outcome observations**

Use this **constructed access packet**, not captured traffic or an executed login
system. The report service's policy permits anyone to read the public catalog.
Only staff assigned to a workshop may download that workshop's restricted roster.
The example backend context is NestJS with the Fastify adapter; no framework
mechanics or runtime installation is needed to read the decisions.

| Attempt | Identity evidence | Authorization result | Recorded operation outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: public catalog read | No account authentication | Allowed by public-read rule | Catalog response completed |
| B: roster W1 download by account `staff-b` | Authentication passed for `staff-b` | Denied: account assigned to W2, not W1 | Denial recorded; roster not returned |
| C: roster W1 download by account `staff-a` | Authentication passed for `staff-a` | Allowed: account assigned to W1 | Allow decision recorded; no completion record supplied |
| D: request claiming name `staff-a` | Only the name string supplied; no accepted authentication evidence | No permission result supplied | An incoming request record contains the claimed name |

For A, lack of authentication does not imply a policy failure. The public rule
permits that action. For B, resetting the password is not a demonstrated repair:
the identity check succeeded, while the workshop assignment did not grant the
requested access. Whether the assignment should change is a policy or business
question, not a conclusion to infer from a user's frustration.

For C, an allowed decision is useful evidence, but completion is unknown. The
client could disconnect or another operation could fail afterward. Accounting
should distinguish the permission decision from a completed transfer when that
outcome matters. For D, the record proves only that a request claimed that name.
Do not attribute the action to a verified account merely because a log field is
called `user`.

A useful rewrite of “Auth is broken” is:

> In attempt B, account `staff-b` authenticated successfully. The roster W1
> request was denied under the stated workshop-assignment policy because the
> account is assigned to W2. The supplied evidence shows a permission decision;
> it does not show an authentication failure. Confirm whether W1 access is
> actually intended before changing the assignment or policy.

Now add an approved W1 assignment for `staff-b`, with a later request evaluated
after the service has received that policy change. An allowed result for that new
request would be consistent with the revised context. It does not make the old
denial incorrect under the old assignment, nor prove every enforcement point
received the change. A policy document update alone is not evidence of actual
runtime enforcement.

Practice: for C, add a record that the response completed and contains the expected
roster. What changes? You now have completion evidence for that response as well
as the permission decision. You still have not established that the human read
or understood the downloaded file, or that every other roster is protected.

### 4) **Common confusion**

Authentication is not synonymous with real-world identity proofing, and possession
of valid authentication evidence is not unlimited authority. Conversely, denying
an authenticated user can be exactly what the intended authorization policy requires.
State the resource and operation before calling a denial a defect.

Browser CORS permission is a separate response-sharing boundary. An allowed origin
is not a verified account or permission to access every record. Similarly, a
client-provided username and an accepted authentication result are different
observations, even if both are recorded in a field labeled identity.

### 5) **Performance note**

Authentication verification, policy evaluation and recording perform different
work. Their costs depend on mechanisms, remote dependencies, request rate and
storage. Reusing prior identity or policy information can change both cost and
freshness, so any caching decision needs its own scope and revocation requirements.
No login latency, policy throughput or logging overhead was measured here.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to identify the claim, accepted evidence, policy decision and
completed outcome for each row. Then remove C's completion record after adding
it: the answer should return to an unknown transfer outcome without changing the
known permission decision. For B, reward a question about the intended workshop
assignment rather than an automatic password reset or blanket access grant.
