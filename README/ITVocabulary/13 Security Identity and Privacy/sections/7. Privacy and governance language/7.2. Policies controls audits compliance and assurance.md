## 7.2. Policies controls audits compliance and assurance

A supplier says “we have a policy, passed an audit and are certified.” Each claim
needs an object and a scope. A policy states expectations; controls put protections
into practice; an assessment gathers evidence about them. None of those words
alone proves that every system is free of security or privacy problems.

Read the earlier access-control, risk and privacy lessons first. This page uses
an original fictional assessment packet. It does not conduct an audit, determine
legal compliance or issue a certification.

### 1) **Connect a rule to its implementation and evidence**

A **policy** states an organization's intended rules and responsibilities. A
**procedure** describes how particular work is carried out. An internal standard
may define required baselines, while a guideline may offer recommended approaches;
check the organization's own document hierarchy and authority. A title alone
does not establish whether a document is mandatory, current or applicable.

For example, a policy can require that catalog workers have no private-roster
access. A procedure can tell an owner how to enumerate worker accounts, compare
their permissions with approved work and record required changes. The existence
of either document does not establish the permissions currently enforced.

A **control** is a measure used to meet an objective or manage risk. Controls can
involve technical mechanisms, organizational activities and physical protections.
A server permission check, an access-review activity and a locked equipment area
serve different purposes. Tie each control to a stated concern rather than counting
how many appear on a checklist.

A **control objective** states what the measure is meant to achieve. A design can
be appropriate on paper while implementation or operation differs. Conversely,
a perfectly executed activity can be inadequate for its intended objective.
Reviewing an incomplete account list consistently would not cover the omitted
accounts. NIST's [control catalog overview](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final)
distinguishes the functions provided from confidence in the capability. Its
catalog is not a mandate to apply every listed control to every organization.

**Evidence** is information supporting an assessment conclusion. A procedure,
configuration snapshot, access decision, review record and observed test have
different subjects and limits. Name the system, version, time and method. A screenshot
showing one setting enabled does not demonstrate every request or a full quarter
of operation.

### 2) **Distinguish assessment confidence from universal safety**

A **control assessment** evaluates measures against stated objectives or criteria.
It can examine documents and mechanisms, interview people, or test behavior under
specified conditions. NIST's [assessment methodology](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-53Ar5.pdf)
distinguishes those methods and their depth and coverage. Reading a test plan is
not executing its test, and interviewing an owner does not by itself reproduce
the claimed operational result.

An **audit** systematically evaluates evidence against defined criteria with
appropriate independence and documentation. It may be internal or external;
external is not part of the word's universal definition. NIST's
[audit glossary](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/audit) records these contexts.
Ask what was audited, by whom, over what period, using which sampling and what
findings or limitations remain. A **finding** states an assessment result; a
**nonconformity** identifies a failure to meet a specified requirement. Missing
information can also leave a conclusion unverified without establishing that
every underlying activity failed.

**Compliance** concerns meeting applicable requirements. Those requirements might
come from law, contracts, an adopted standard or internal policy. “Compliant”
therefore needs the requirements, jurisdiction where relevant, subject and time.
Passing one internal access review is not a determination of every legal obligation.
**Assurance** is justified confidence from the evidence and assessment basis,
not an absolute guarantee that no incident can happen.

A **certification** is an independent body's written assurance that a specified
subject meets particular requirements. **Accreditation** concerns formal recognition
of the certification body's competence under the relevant system. ISO's
[certification explanation](https://www.iso.org/certification.html) distinguishes
them and states that ISO itself does not issue certificates. Verify the issuer,
standard and edition, certified scope, validity and applicable conditions instead
of relying on a logo.

For a common example, [ISO/IEC 27001:2022](https://www.iso.org/standard/27001) defines
requirements for an **information security management system**, or **ISMS**.
A certification claim concerns that management system within its stated scope.
It does not assert that every line of a company's product code has no defects
or that every service the company owns was included. Implementing a standard
and obtaining certification to it are also separate claims.

**SOC**, **System and Organization Controls**, is another context for assurance
reporting. A **SOC 2 report** concerns an examination of controls at a service
organization relevant to specified trust-services criteria. The
[AICPA's SOC resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/audit-assurance/audit-and-assurance-greater-than-soc-2)
describe these reporting services. Read the actual report's system, criteria,
type, covered time and results. A marketing badge is not the report and does not
supply an unlimited guarantee. This lesson does not interpret a particular SOC
opinion or replace its detailed examination and reporting requirements.

### 3) **Review a fictional assurance packet without expanding its scope**

Use this **constructed packet**, with no unstated exception approvals:

| Record | Supplied fact |
|---|---|
| Internal policy P-4 | Catalog-worker accounts may write public catalog files; private-roster reads require separately justified authority |
| Access-review procedure | Inventory worker accounts, compare permissions with approved work, document and verify necessary corrections |
| Population | Application A has 60 active worker accounts |
| Submitted review evidence | Covers 10 named accounts for the July–September review period; no evidence for the other 50 is supplied |
| Nine reviewed accounts | Their inspected grants match the approved public-catalog work |
| Tenth account W | Has private-roster read permission; the complete supplied approval record states no justification or exception for it |
| Correction record | Owner opened a removal ticket for W; no changed-permission or enforcement result supplied |
| Certification scope | The supplied certificate covers a specified ISMS for Application A operations; Application B is excluded |

The packet supports a permission mismatch for W under P-4. The correction ticket
records intended work, not completion. Obtain the changed configuration and
appropriate enforcement evidence before saying the unnecessary authority ended.
A control design and a plan to repair it are different from effective operation.

The nine matching grants support the stated inspection results for those accounts.
They do not prove that all 60 matched policy, that every request was enforced
correctly, or that those grants were unchanged throughout the quarter. For the
other 50, the packet lacks review evidence; it does not establish whether reviews
actually occurred elsewhere. State that limitation and request the missing scope.

Application B is outside the stated certificate scope. The certificate therefore
cannot supply the missing assurance claim about B. That does not prove B is
insecure, and W's finding does not by itself determine the certificate's formal
status. Those questions require their own criteria, assessment and authority.

Now add a time-stamped correction record and an authorized check showing that W's
roster request is denied after its unnecessary permission was removed. This
strengthens the correction claim for W at that time and tested boundary. It does
not verify the other 50 accounts, earlier operation, every alternate route or B.

Practice: replace the review records with a policy PDF and an unsigned statement
saying “all checks passed.” Can the same account-level conclusions be retained?
No. The policy remains a criterion, while the statement lacks the specific
observations and assessment basis supplied by the original records. Ask what was
examined, interviewed or tested before describing the work as verified.

A useful review asks for the requirement, control, implementation, evidence,
assessment scope, conclusion, owner and unresolved action. That chain connects
governance language to actual work without treating a finding as either meaningless
paperwork or automatic proof of every possible risk.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A policy is not its enforcement mechanism, and an audit trail is not an audit.
An activity log can support an assessment, but someone must evaluate its relevance,
reliability and completeness against the criteria. Similarly, a completed ticket
needs outcome evidence when the claim concerns changed permissions.

Compliance, certification and security are related but different claims. A scoped
assessment can provide useful confidence while leaving other systems, times or
failure modes unexamined. An excluded system is unassessed by that evidence,
not automatically safe or automatically compromised.

### 5) **Performance note**

Governance labels have no direct runtime-speed effect. Controls such as request
checks and logging can affect cost; evidence collection, interviews and testing
consume people and system resources too. Their value and overhead depend on the
objective and implementation rather than the number of policies or badges.

No audit, control execution, permission change, certificate verification, SOC
examination or performance measurement was performed here. Sampling and time
limits in the fictional packet are reasoning inputs, not observed coverage of a
real system.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to draw separate boxes for criterion, mechanism, observed evidence
and assessment conclusion. Give them the permission ticket and ask which box it
belongs in. A strong answer keeps intended correction separate from verified
outcome, preserves the 10-of-60 limit and refuses to extend A's certificate to B.
Then add W's denial evidence and require a precisely bounded revised statement.
