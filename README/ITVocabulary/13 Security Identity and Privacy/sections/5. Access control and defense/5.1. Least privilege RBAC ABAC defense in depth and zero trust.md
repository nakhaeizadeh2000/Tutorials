## 5.1. Least privilege RBAC ABAC defense in depth and zero trust

“Only staff can access the API” leaves several questions unanswered. Which staff
member, which operation, which workshop and under which conditions? Being on an
internal network does not supply those missing permissions. Access-control terms
become useful when they help you describe an actual decision.

Read the identity and principal lessons first. Here the subject is the policy
being enforced, not a new login mechanism. The examples use a fictional workshop
service with NestJS and the Fastify adapter as context; no service or policy engine
is running.

### 1) **Describe privileges through subjects actions resources and conditions**

**Access control** restricts operations according to policy. In a decision, the
**subject** is the acting entity, the **resource** or object is what it wants to
access, and the **action** is the requested operation. The same account may be
allowed to read one roster but denied another, or allowed to read a report but
denied its deletion. An authenticated identity is an input, not the entire decision.

**Least privilege** means granting only the authority necessary for the intended
work, with the relevant resource and duration limits. It does not mean preventing
people from doing their jobs or assuming that a higher job title requires every
permission. A catalog-generation worker may need public-file writes while having
no need to read private attendee details. Review the work and its dependencies
before granting a broad administrative role merely to remove an error.

**Role-based access control**, or **RBAC**, associates permissions with roles and
assigns subjects to those roles. A role could group roster-reading capabilities
for instructors. The role name is a convenient grouping; its actual permissions
and assignment rules determine what it grants. A role called `instructor` does
not inherently mean access to every workshop in every organization.

**Attribute-based access control**, or **ABAC**, evaluates attributes against a
policy. Relevant attributes can describe the subject, resource, requested action
and environment. NIST's [ABAC definition](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/162/upd2/final)
includes those categories and relationships. A rule might require that the
instructor's assigned-workshop attribute match the roster's workshop and that
the request occur during an authorized period. Roles can participate as attributes;
a system can combine these models rather than choosing one label for every rule.

AWS's [ABAC explanation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/introduction_attribute-based-access-control.html)
provides a concrete principal-tag/resource-tag comparison. That is one implementation
context, not a universal requirement to use tags or AWS IAM roles. A tag also needs
a trustworthy source and controlled modification: a requester choosing its own
trusted department would undermine a policy that relies on that department.
ABAC does not automatically guarantee accurate attributes or immediate updates.

The original [web-security discussion](https://martinfowler.com/articles/web-security-basics.html)
connects permissions to particular actions and resources instead of hard-coded
usernames. Apply that question before selecting a model. RBAC can make recurring
responsibilities easier to manage; attributes can express changing context and
resource relationships. Both need understandable rules, ownership and checks.
Neither acronym alone proves an appropriate or correctly enforced policy.

### 2) **Distinguish layered protection from implicit trust**

**Deny by default** means the system rejects a request unless its policy supplies
a permitting rule under the required conditions. A public catalog can have an
explicit rule allowing anonymous reads. Public access therefore does not contradict
deny by default. An unknown or missing required attribute must not silently become
an allow result in a design that depends on that attribute.

**Enforcement** is where the decision actually controls the operation. Hiding a
button can improve the interface, but a client can send a request without that
button. The service must enforce the relevant action/resource rule. OWASP's
[authorization guidance](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Authorization_Cheat_Sheet.html#validate-the-permissions-on-every-request)
emphasizes checking each request. A check on the usual web path does not establish
protection of an alternate export route, background operation or static file.
Full enforcement architecture and framework integration remain specialist study.

**Defense in depth** combines protections so one failure does not become the only
barrier to harm. For a private roster, transport protection, resource authorization,
restricted storage access and suitable monitoring address different paths and
stages. Their value depends on the actual failure scenario and dependencies.
Two screens hiding the same button do not provide independent server-side checks.

OWASP's [storage defense discussion](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Cryptographic_Storage_Cheat_Sheet.html#defense-in-depth)
explains why encryption does not remove access-control obligations. An application
that can legitimately decrypt records may still disclose them to the wrong caller
if it skips authorization. Adding another layer does not excuse leaving an existing
critical rule incorrect, and an alert is not the same as preventing disclosure.

**Zero trust** removes implicit trust based solely on network location or asset
ownership and focuses protection on resources and evaluated access. NIST's
[Zero Trust Architecture](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/207/final) establishes
that orientation. “Inside our VPN” or “company laptop” is not by itself authority
to export every roster. Network and device context can still be relevant inputs;
they simply do not replace all other checks.

Zero trust is not a promise to trust no evidence, a requirement to ask for a password
on every packet, or a guarantee supplied by buying one proxy. A zero-trust claim
needs the actual identity, resource policy, enforcement boundaries and handling of
changed context. RBAC and ABAC describe policy organization, while defense in depth
and zero trust describe broader protection choices; the terms are not synonyms.

### 3) **Apply a policy to a bounded request matrix**

Use this **constructed policy**, with no unstated administrative exceptions:

1. Anyone may read the public catalog.
2. An authenticated instructor may read a private roster only for a workshop
   currently assigned to that instructor and only during the open review period.
3. The authenticated catalog worker may write generated public catalog files.
4. All other operations are denied. Required missing or unverified attributes
   do not satisfy a permitting condition.

The packet states that trusted policy data identifies instructor Ada's assignment
as W1. W2 is another workshop. The worker has its own principal. The period state
is supplied separately for each independent request below.

| Request | Relevant evidence | Decision under this policy |
|---|---|---|
| Anonymous read of public catalog | Public resource and read action | Allow by rule 1 |
| Ada reads W1 roster | Identity accepted; instructor role; assignment W1; period open | Allow by rule 2 |
| Ada reads W2 roster | Same accepted identity and role; assignment remains W1; period open | Deny: resource relationship fails |
| Ada reads W1 roster outside the period | Accepted identity, role and assignment; period closed | Deny: environmental condition fails |
| Catalog worker reads W1 roster | Accepted worker identity; no instructor permission | Deny: worker write rule does not permit roster reads |
| Catalog worker writes a generated public catalog file | Accepted worker identity and the specified operation/resource | Allow by rule 3 |

The role supplies a recurring responsibility; the assignment and period add
resource/context conditions. This is a mixed policy, not proof that one model
is universally superior. Each denied row can have valid authentication. Moving
the W2 request to the internal network would not satisfy its missing assignment.

Now change one fact: the authoritative assignment changes to W2 and the decision
process receives and uses that update before a new request during the open period.
Under the same policy, the new W2 read is allowed and a new W1 read is denied.
An earlier W1 result does not become retroactively incorrect. Updating a directory
without evidence that the decision used the update would leave enforcement
uncertain; the model name ABAC cannot fill that gap.

Practice: remove the trusted assignment data and replace it with “the browser
submits workshop W1.” Can rule 2's assignment requirement be treated as satisfied?
No. The requested resource is not evidence that the subject is assigned to it.
Under the stated missing-attribute rule, the roster request is denied. Ask where
the accepted assignment comes from before claiming the authorization design works.

These are explained outcomes of the supplied policy, not results from running an
API, permission test or network access attempt. A policy document alone does not
verify that a deployed endpoint implements these decisions.

### 4) **Common confusion**

“Administrator,” “internal” and “authenticated” are not universal allow rules.
They need a scope and policy. An interface restriction is also different from
service enforcement, and a resource identifier's obscurity is different from
checking whether this subject may access that resource.

ABAC does not mean “no roles,” and RBAC does not require one permission for every
operation in the system. A hybrid policy can be reasonable. The important question
is whether its actual attributes, assignments and enforcement support the required
decisions without unintended grants.

### 5) **Performance note**

Policy evaluation can require local data, a remote decision, resource lookups or
cached attributes. Cache lifetime affects both cost and how quickly changed
assignments reach decisions. The matrix assumes updates were received and used;
it does not measure that propagation or prescribe a universal caching rule.

RBAC, ABAC and zero-trust labels provide no latency ranking. Added checks and
monitoring also consume resources, but skipping a required authorization decision
changes behavior rather than preserving it as a mere optimization. No policy,
network or access-check benchmark was performed here.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to justify every allow with a specific rule and every denial
with the missing condition. Then move the denied W2 request inside a VPN and
ask them to evaluate it again. A sound answer retains the resource restriction.
Finally, remove the assignment's trusted source and require the learner to
separate the browser's request from the service's authorization evidence.
