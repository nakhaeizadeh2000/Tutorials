## 4.2. Symmetric asymmetric keys certificates trust and key management

A team renews a certificate and announces that its encryption keys have been
rotated. Another team changes the key used for new backups and deletes the old
one. Neither action's consequences can be understood from the word “key” alone.
Identify the operation, the key material and every remaining dependency on it.

Read the preceding cryptographic-purpose lesson first. The earlier TLS lesson
owns service-name matching and channel endpoints; this lesson connects those
ideas to general key roles, trust and lifecycle decisions. All records below are
constructed. No keys, certificates, trust stores or cloud resources are changed.

### 1) **Match key material to the cryptographic operation**

A **cryptographic key** is material that controls an operation under a scheme.
A key's **identifier** or friendly name refers to that material; it is not
necessarily the material itself. A managed service can retain one logical key
identifier while changing the version used for new operations. Ask whether a
record describes the identifier, an algorithm, a version or actual secret bytes.

**Symmetric-key** cryptography uses shared secret key material. In symmetric
encryption, the same secret key supports encryption and decryption. A shared
HMAC key supports tag generation and verification instead. These are different
purposes even when both involve symmetric secrets. Knowledge of a key does not
justify reusing it for unrelated operations or protocols.

**Asymmetric**, or **public-key**, cryptography uses related public and private
key material with different roles. A private signing key generates signatures;
the public verification key checks them. In a public-key encryption scheme,
public encryption material and private decryption material serve another purpose.
A key-agreement scheme instead lets parties establish shared key material under
its protocol. Not every public key supports all of those operations.

**Key establishment** concerns getting suitable shared key material into the
participants' possession. A **key-encapsulation mechanism**, or **KEM**, is one
kind of key-establishment scheme. It is not simply another name for encrypting
an entire report. Systems can combine public-key establishment with symmetric
encryption for the actual data; the mechanisms cooperate rather than compete
as one universally better category.

Current vocabulary includes standardized **post-quantum cryptography**: schemes
designed to resist relevant attacks by quantum computers. NIST finalized
[FIPS 203 for ML-KEM](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/203/final),
[FIPS 204 for ML-DSA](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/204/final) and
[FIPS 205 for SLH-DSA](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/205/final) on August 13, 2024.
ML-KEM establishes shared secrets; ML-DSA and SLH-DSA provide digital signatures.
Their common security context does not make their functions interchangeable.
A published standard also does not establish that a particular client, service
or certificate workflow supports it. “Post-quantum” alone proves neither correct
integration nor protection against every kind of attack.

### 2) **Separate public information from an accepted trust relationship**

A **certificate** supplies an issuer-signed binding involving a public key and
subject information under a defined certificate system. It is not the subject's
private key. Making the certificate public does not require publishing the private
key. A file called `certificate-and-key` might contain several objects, however;
inspect its actual contents and intended handling rather than classifying a whole
file from one word in its name.

**Public key infrastructure**, or **PKI**, includes the participants, policies
and mechanisms used to issue, distribute, validate and manage these bindings.
A CA's signature is evidence within this system, not a universal statement that
every program should trust the certificate for every use. A **trust store** holds
trusted material used by a client or application; the **trust anchor** is the
trusted starting information for a particular validation path.

[RFC 5280's path-validation model](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5280.html#section-6)
connects a subject/public-key binding to a configured trust anchor. The earlier
TLS lesson explains the additional intended-service checks. A valid path does
not replace the application's resource permissions, and accepting a different
anchor changes a trust decision. Adding an unknown issuer merely to silence an
error can therefore expand what the client accepts.

A **self-signed certificate** is signed using the private key corresponding to
its own public key. That signature alone does not establish an external trusted
identity. Nor does “self-signed” automatically mean unusable: a system may
explicitly provision trusted starting material. What matters is the independent
trust arrangement and the applicable validation rules, not a circular claim that
the received object says it should be trusted.

**Certificate renewal** obtains a replacement certificate. **Key rotation**
changes the key material used for a defined purpose. Renewal can accompany a new
key pair or reuse an existing one, depending on the process. A newer expiration
date alone does not prove a new private key, and generating a new pair does not
prove every server or client has adopted it. Certificate revocation similarly
needs an applicable checking and enforcement mechanism; publishing a revocation
record does not describe every client's actual behavior.

### 3) **Trace generation replacement and recovery obligations**

**Key management** covers the lifecycle: generation, controlled distribution,
access and use, storage, replacement, recovery where appropriate, and eventual
destruction. A **cryptoperiod** is the authorized usage period for a key in a
specified role. The period for creating new protected data and the period for
processing existing data can differ. A rotation schedule is a policy input;
it is not proof that replacement or recovery occurred.

The [OWASP key-management guide](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Key_Management_Cheat_Sheet.html)
connects these responsibilities to the application's actual objectives. Keep a
record of each key's purpose, owner, version, authorized users and dependent data.
A key-management service (**KMS**) can control key operations and access. A
hardware security module (**HSM**) supplies a protected cryptographic processing
boundary. These facilities still require suitable policy and integration;
their names do not establish who may request decryption or whether recovery works.

In **envelope encryption**, a **data encryption key**, or **DEK**, encrypts data,
while a **key encryption key**, or **KEK**, protects the data key. Changing the
wrapping key is different from re-encrypting every byte of the report. Ask what
was rewrapped, what was re-encrypted and what old material remains necessary.
AWS's [rotation documentation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/rotate-keys.html)
provides a concrete managed example: it distinguishes current material for new
encryption from retained material used to decrypt older ciphertext. That behavior
is scoped to the documented arrangement, not every product called KMS.

Read this **fictional key inventory and change packet**. `K1`, `K2`, `T1` and
`T2` are labels, not actual key values.

| Record | Supplied fact |
|---|---|
| Backup A | Directly encrypted with symmetric key K1; no envelope scheme or alternative recovery copy |
| Current writer | Now encrypts new backups with K2 |
| Key inventory | K1 retained for authorized old-backup decryption; K2 active for new encryption |
| Recovery observation | An authorized recovery process successfully decrypted A using K1 |
| Certificate change | A replacement TLS certificate was issued for the existing public key T1 |
| Service observation | The inspected server still uses the corresponding private key for T1 |

The backup encryption role has changed for new writes. Backup A has not thereby
been re-encrypted with K2. The supplied recovery result supports recovery of A
with K1 under that observation's conditions. It does not verify every backup,
all application restoration steps or recovery time.

The TLS certificate was renewed while the service key pair remained the same.
That record does not demonstrate TLS key rotation. It also concerns a different
purpose from K1/K2 storage protection. Calling both actions “we refreshed the
keys” would hide the distinction needed for review.

Now change only the inventory: K1 and every recoverable copy of its material are
destroyed before A is re-encrypted, with no other recovery arrangement. The
packet no longer supplies the material required to decrypt A. Possessing K2 or
A's checksum does not replace K1. The old successful recovery does not establish
recovery after destruction. Assess the dependencies before destroying material;
this is an explanation of the failure, not an instruction to delete keys.

Practice: instead, issue a certificate for new public key T2, leaving the inspected
server on T1. Has this observation established adoption of T2 by that server?
No. Issuance and deployment are separate steps. Obtain evidence of the relevant
service's configuration and presented identity. Do not silently change the expected
identity to whichever certificate happens to appear.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A public key can be public while still requiring integrity and a trustworthy
association with its intended owner. Secrecy and trust are different requirements.
A key identifier, certificate and private key are also different objects even
when a management console displays them on the same screen.

Rotation does not automatically revoke every prior credential, re-encrypt stored
data, replace every certificate or undo earlier disclosure. A compromise response
must examine the affected purposes and dependencies. Avoid treating one successful
key change as proof that previously exposed plaintext became secret again.

### 5) **Performance note**

Cryptographic operations, remote key-service calls, certificate validation and
large-data re-encryption have different costs. Envelope encryption distinguishes
processing data keys from processing whole datasets, but it does not provide a
universal latency or migration-time estimate. Availability of the key service
and authorized recovery material can also affect service availability.

No key generation, service call, rewrapping, validation or recovery benchmark was
performed here. The packet's recovery is fictional evidence to interpret. Key
names and algorithm categories alone cannot rank the arrangements' speed.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Give the learner separate columns for key identifier, material version, purpose,
new-operation permission and old-data dependency. Have them place K1/K2 and T1/T2
in those columns, then remove K1. A strong answer explains the recovery dependency
without confusing it with certificate renewal. Follow with a new self-signed
certificate and ask what independent fact would justify trusting it.
