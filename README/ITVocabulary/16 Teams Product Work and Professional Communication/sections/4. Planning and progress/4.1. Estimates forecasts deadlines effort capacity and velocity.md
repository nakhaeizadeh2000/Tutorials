## 4.1. Estimates forecasts deadlines effort capacity and velocity

“The story is eight points, so it will finish in eight hours.” This changes a
relative estimate into a clock without evidence. Useful planning states the
quantity, unit, conditions and uncertainty before attaching a delivery date.
The calculations below use fictional inputs; they predict no actual team's work.

### 1) **State what quantity a planning number describes**

**Effort** is an amount of human work. An estimate of twelve person-hours might
include implementation, review and checking. It does not mean the result will
be available twelve wall-clock hours after a request arrives. **Elapsed duration**
includes the passage of time between defined start and finish events, including
waiting and time when nobody works on that item.

If one person works four hours on Monday, waits two days for a decision and works
another four hours on Thursday, the recorded effort is eight person-hours under
that accounting. The elapsed duration spans several days. Adding people does not
necessarily divide duration: dependencies, specialist knowledge and coordination
can constrain what proceeds together.

An **estimate** approximates a specified quantity, such as effort, size or duration.
A **forecast** predicts a future outcome, such as a delivery range, using evidence
and assumptions. Teams sometimes use the words interchangeably; identify the
underlying question. A **deadline** is a required or agreed latest time. A
**commitment** is an undertaking to deliver a stated outcome under an agreement.
The deadline does not prove feasibility, and the forecast does not automatically
create a commitment.

**Team capacity** concerns available ability and time for the work in a period.
Calendar availability, leave, support duties, skills and shared reviewers matter.
Five people times eight hours is not automatically forty uninterrupted hours for
one feature. Likewise, service capacity measured in requests per second is a
different concept from a team's available work capacity.

A useful planning statement says: “We estimate eight to twelve person-hours of
implementation and checking, assuming the provider field is available. The delivery
forecast also depends on its approval and review queue. The requested Friday
deadline has not been reconciled with that dependency.” Preserve what is known
and ask for the decision that matters rather than choosing a falsely precise date.

### 2) **Use team history without turning points into a universal clock**

**Story points** are team-relative estimates of item size or effort under a shared
convention. Teams may consider amount of work, complexity and uncertainty. They
have no universal hours-per-point conversion. Mountain Goat Software's original
[story-point teaching guide](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/story-points/introducing-story-points-to-a-team)
uses reference items to explain relative estimation and distinguishes forecasting
from evaluation of people. That method is an option, not a requirement imposed
by Scrum or a guarantee that relative estimates are always more accurate.

**Velocity** commonly totals the estimates of work completed during an iteration
under the team's finish rule. Agile Alliance's [velocity explanation](https://agilealliance.org/glossary/velocity/)
also distinguishes this observed total from a target or forecast. If completed
items have estimates three and five, while an eight-point item is unfinished,
velocity is eight under this convention, not sixteen and not a percentage-based
credit for the unfinished item. Partial progress still matters to investigation;
it simply does not satisfy that completed-work counting rule.

History can inform a forecast when units, work mix, finish criteria and operating
conditions remain sufficiently comparable. Changing the point scale, item breakdown,
team availability, dependencies or completion standard weakens a mechanical
comparison. Re-estimate or update the model rather than hiding those changes.
One team's twenty points and another's forty do not establish that the second
team delivers twice the value or has twice the productivity.

Raw completed-item **throughput** is another work-flow measure, distinct from
point totals and from application request throughput. Neither completed-item count
nor velocity alone measures user benefit. Splitting one task into ten cards can
change a counter without improving the product. Keep the outcome beside the metric.

### 3) **Challenge a date claim using its actual premises**

Use this constructed packet. An iteration is two weeks, and an item counts only
when implementation and required review are complete.

| Record | Supplied information |
|---|---|
| Last iteration | Items A3 and B5 completed; item C8 still awaits review |
| Earlier comparable iterations | Completed-point totals 8 and 12 |
| Remaining scope | 24 points in the same scale, including C8; no scope change assumed |
| Availability | Next iteration has one fewer available specialist; effect is unmeasured |
| Dependency | Provider approval is required for one remaining item and has no agreed date |
| Request | Stakeholder asks for completion within four weeks |
| Message | “Average velocity is ten; 24 divided by ten guarantees delivery in four weeks” |

The last total is 3 + 5 = 8, so the three-iteration history is 8, 12, 8. Its arithmetic
mean is 28/3, about 9.33 points per iteration. Even the message's assumed ten would
give 24/10 = 2.4 iterations, or 4.8 weeks in a continuous-rate illustration. It does
not establish four weeks. Under a simpler model that counts complete iterations,
finishing at most twelve points each iteration would require at least two, and
finishing eight each would require three. Those are conditional scenarios, not
statistical confidence bounds or guarantees about the next two or three iterations.

More importantly, the missing provider date and changed specialist availability
prevent the arithmetic from establishing actual completion. A model cannot infer
that the dependency clears merely because a velocity number exists. C8 is already
included in the remaining 24; adding it again would double count the same scope.

A precise response is: “The recorded completed totals are 8, 12 and 8. The four-week
request remains a deadline to assess, not a demonstrated forecast. We need the
provider decision date and an assessment of specialist availability, then can
revise scope or timing with the relevant decision owner.” A narrower outcome may
be possible, but its scope needs agreement rather than silent removal of quality work.

Practice: supply a confirmed provider approval date before the next iteration,
leaving specialist availability unchanged. One uncertainty is reduced. The old
mean is still not a guaranteed future rate. If instead the team simply doubles
all point values, reported velocity can double without any evidence of faster delivery.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A range is not automatically a probability interval. “Two to three iterations”
needs assumptions and a method before anyone can attach a confidence percentage.
An estimate becoming outdated is also different from breaking a commitment: first
recover what was actually promised and under which conditions.

Capacity and velocity are not interchangeable. One describes availability and
ability; the other records completed estimates under a convention. The same
number of available people can coexist with different completion outcomes.

### 5) **Performance note**

These planning measures do not directly change runtime latency or throughput.
More points, person-hours or started tasks do not prove better product or delivery
performance. The arithmetic checks a synthetic model only; no team productivity,
forecast accuracy or service-speed study was performed. Treat pressure to raise a
counter separately from improving a measured user or work-flow outcome.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to label each packet number as size, completed total, duration or
deadline. Then ask why the unknown approval date cannot be recovered from the
mean. A sound answer corrects the arithmetic but does not stop there: it keeps
capacity, dependencies, scope and commitment decisions visible.
