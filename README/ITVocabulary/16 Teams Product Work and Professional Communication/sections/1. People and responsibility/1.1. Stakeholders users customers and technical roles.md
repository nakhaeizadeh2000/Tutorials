## 1.1. Stakeholders users customers and technical roles

“Ask the customer; the PM already approved it.” This sentence hides several questions:
who pays, who performs the task, what PM means and what that person can approve.
A useful reply identifies the people and the decision before scheduling more work.
This lesson uses a fictional workshop-registration product; no workplace contact
or product evaluation is required.

### 1) **Distinguish affected people from buyers and users**

A **stakeholder** is a person or group with an interest in, influence on, or exposure
to the work and its outcomes. A **user** performs a task with the product. A
**customer** acquires the product or service, often as a paying individual or
organization. These everyday categories overlap; a contract or framework can use
more specific definitions. Clarify the relevant usage instead of assuming every
speaker applies exactly this taxonomy.

For example, a training company buys a registration service. Its purchasing manager
handles the commercial relationship. Workshop coordinators use its export daily,
and attendees use its booking page. Support staff deal with failed registrations.
The purchaser is not the only person whose needs matter. A coordinator can identify
an unusable export without having authority to change the contract price.

Distinguish **interest**, **expertise** and **authority**. Someone affected by a
change can provide important evidence without holding its release decision. A
person authorized to accept a deliverable still needs evidence about actual users.
Likewise, an external vendor is a stakeholder in an integration discussion without
automatically deciding the application's requirements.

A practical question is: “Whose task are we improving, and who decides the proposed
scope?” Record both answers. Asking only the easiest person to reach can miss the
real task; inviting everyone to approve every detail can create unnecessary waiting.
Select participation according to the decision and its consequences.

### 2) **Interpret role names through actual responsibilities**

The following are common readings, not universal job descriptions:

| Label | Common expansion and focus | Clarification that matters |
|---|---|---|
| PM | Product manager concerned with product problems and priorities; alternatively project manager coordinating a bounded project | Which expansion and which decisions? |
| PO | Product Owner in Scrum | Is the team using Scrum's accountability or a local title? |
| QA | Quality assurance; sometimes a testing team or job label | Which quality work and which acceptance decision? |
| SRE | Site Reliability Engineering or an engineer working in that field | Which service objectives and operational duties? |
| TL | Technical lead or team lead | Technical direction, people management, coordination or some combination? |
| EM | Engineering manager | Which people, staffing, delivery and technical responsibilities are assigned? |
| IC | Individual contributor | What expertise and influence does the position carry without assuming direct reports? |

An IC can be a senior technical leader and mentor. A technical lead does not
necessarily conduct performance reviews. A manager does not automatically own every
technical decision. GitLab's [published role/responsibility distinction](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/product-development/how-we-work/roles-and-responsibilities/#role-vs-responsibility)
illustrates why one person can cover several responsibility areas; its organization
chart is one company's arrangement. Ask for the local responsibilities.

Scrum gives **Product Owner** a specific accountability for product value and
Product Backlog management. The [2020 Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html#product-owner)
allows delegation of that management work while retaining accountability. This
does not define all companies' product-manager positions or give the PO unlimited
technical, contractual or employment authority. A person may hold both a job title
and a framework accountability; establish each separately.

QA activities and SRE practices already have detailed owners in this track. Use
those explanations rather than treating QA as “the manual testers” or SRE as
“whoever receives every alert.” A role label does not establish the work performed.

### 3) **Route a product question to the relevant people**

Read this constructed packet:

| Record | Supplied information |
|---|---|
| Buyer | TrainingCo buys the service; purchaser Mina manages the contract |
| Users | Coordinators need a list of pending bookings to contact before each workshop |
| Local roles | Ravi's PM title means product manager; Jo is technical lead and an IC; Lee manages the engineers |
| Scope authority | TrainingCo assigns coordinator Ada to accept the pending-contact workflow |
| Existing evidence | Jo checked that the current export contains approved bookings only, as its written rule requires |
| Message | Ravi writes “PM approved the export, so customer acceptance is complete” |

The implementation check addresses the approved-only rule. It does not establish
the pending-contact workflow or Ada's acceptance. Ravi's title does not override
the supplied acceptance assignment. Mina can clarify commercial implications,
Ada and representative coordinators can explain the workflow, Jo can explain
implementation choices, and Lee can address staffing constraints if needed.
No one needs to infer a new hierarchy from these facts.

A useful response is: “The checked export follows its approved-only rule. The
pending-contact task needs a different selection or an additional view. Ada owns
acceptance for that workflow under this assignment. Ravi and Ada should reconcile
its scope with coordinator needs; Jo can assess the technical options.” This
preserves the successful check while making the unresolved decision explicit.

Practice: add Ada's acceptance of an independently checked pending-booking view.
Must the old export now be changed? No: the task may be served by the additional
view while the original export retains its separate purpose. State the accepted
view and checked conditions. Acceptance does not prove every user task or future
version works. If the assignment row is removed instead, acceptance authority
becomes unknown; Ravi's title alone does not fill that gap.

### 4) **Common confusion**

“User,” “customer” and “stakeholder” are not three ranks of decision power. They
answer different questions about use, acquisition and involvement. A person can
occupy several categories. A software user account is an identity record, whereas
the user here is the person performing a task.

An acronym expansion also does not settle authority. “PM approved” remains
incomplete even after expanding product manager: identify the approved object,
criteria and assigned decision. Avoid interpreting IC as junior or isolated work.

### 5) **Performance note**

Titles and stakeholder categories have no direct effect on program execution.
They can help locate the person who knows a workload or acceptable response time.
Calling an export “fast enough for the customer” still needs the intended user
task, input size, environment and measured outcome. This packet contains no timing
or productivity measurements, and adding an approval meeting guarantees no speedup.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to route three questions: which records staff need, whether the
filter implements its rule, and who can accept the workflow. A strong answer names
different evidence and possible participants without inventing a hierarchy. Then
remove the authority row. The learner should ask for an assignment rather than
promote a familiar job title into a decision right.
