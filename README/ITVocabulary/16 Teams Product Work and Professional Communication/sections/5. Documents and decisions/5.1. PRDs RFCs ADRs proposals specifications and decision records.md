## 5.1. PRDs RFCs ADRs proposals specifications and decision records

“The RFC is merged, so the feature is live.” A merged document can establish a
recorded decision under a team's process. It does not establish the running
software. Read document purpose, authority, status and implementation evidence as
separate questions. This lesson extends the earlier document-reading guide into
product and architecture work.

### 1) **Choose a document from its purpose and authority**

A **product requirements document**, or **PRD**, describes the product problem,
intended users, scope and required outcomes under the team's process. It can record
assumptions, exclusions and success criteria as well as functions. Atlassian's
[PRD explanation](https://www.atlassian.com/agile/product-management/requirements/)
provides one practical account; its software and template are not required. A PRD
may link detailed specifications and evaluation records rather than contain every
implementation instruction. Its presence alone does not establish agreement.

A **proposal** suggests a course of action. An internal **Request for Comments**,
or **RFC**, commonly invites review of a proposed change. The team defines who
contributes, who decides, which status records that decision and whether merging
means acceptance. “RFC” is not itself an approval rule. An accepted internal RFC
can remain a useful historical record even after the decision changes.

This internal usage differs from the Internet RFC publication series. The
[RFC Editor's overview](https://www.rfc-editor.org/series/rfc/)
explains that published RFCs have statuses and relationships such as updates or
obsoletions; not all are Internet Standards. An internal file named RFC-12 does
not acquire an Internet publication status. Use the earlier document owner for
normative versus explanatory material and version-specific reading.

A **specification** defines behavior, requirements or interfaces in its declared
scope. It can be part of a PRD, a separate contract or an implementation-specific
reference. A specification may still be a draft. A file's “spec” title cannot
resolve whether it was accepted or whether the system conforms to it.

A **decision record** preserves what was decided, the context, rationale, relevant
alternatives and consequences. An **architecture decision record**, or **ADR**,
focuses on an architecturally significant choice. Michael Nygard's original
[ADR account](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions)
explains retaining rationale so later maintainers can assess a choice rather than
blindly accept or reverse it. His particular path and file numbering are one
practice, not a universal mandatory format.

For example, a PRD can establish the need for selected-workshop exports. An internal
RFC can propose immediate generation versus queued processing. An ADR can record
which approach was selected and why. A precise interface specification can define
pending versus completed responses. None substitutes for evidence that the candidate
implementation produces the specified outcomes.

### 2) **Read decision state separately from implementation state**

Common document states include **proposed**, **accepted**, **rejected** and
**superseded**. Interpret them through the local process. A proposed choice awaits
a decision; accepted records the decision; rejected records that it was not chosen;
superseded indicates a later decision replaced it within some scope.

Retain relevant earlier rationale and link to the replacement. Quietly overwriting
“we chose immediate generation” with “we chose a queue” loses the explanation for
old code and historical observations. Keeping an accepted historical decision
without visible replacement navigation can mislead new readers in the other direction.
A current summary can point to both records while clearly naming which applies.

Also track **decision scope**: a new choice for large exports might retain immediate
generation for small ones. Newer is not automatically authoritative for every
operation. Recover the trigger, conditions and authority before declaring two
statements contradictory.

An **implementation status** records whether work was built, checked or deployed.
It is a different dimension from decision status. A rejected alternative might
have a functioning prototype; an accepted choice might have no implementation.
Link evidence to the relevant candidate and environment. A meeting note saying
“approved” still needs the decision object and authorized participants.

### 3) **Reconcile conflicting documents through scope and history**

Read this fictional document packet:

| Artifact | Supplied record |
|---|---|
| PRD P4 | Coordinators need pending contacts for the selected workshop; large-export timing remains unresolved |
| ADR-2 | Immediate generation selected for all exports; later marked superseded by ADR-5 |
| RFC-9 | Proposes queued processing for large exports; local process says merging records the assigned decision owner's acceptance |
| ADR-5 | Accepted: exports above the agreed size threshold use a queue; smaller exports retain immediate generation; links to RFC-9 |
| Candidate H7 | Inspection shows immediate generation only; no queue worker present |
| Status update | “RFC merged; asynchronous export is live and meets the timing requirement” |

ADR-5 is the recorded current choice for large exports under these supplied facts.
ADR-2 remains useful history, but its all-exports rule no longer defines the full
current choice. RFC acceptance is established because the packet explicitly defines
its merge rule; this is not a universal inference from Git merging.

H7's observed implementation does not supply the large-export queue selected in
ADR-5. That is an implementation-to-decision gap for H7, not proof the ADR was
never accepted. The unresolved timing requirement and absence of measurements also
make the “meets timing” claim unsupported. The packet contains no production
inventory, so H7's state should not be silently expanded to every running version.

A useful correction is: “Queued large exports are accepted in ADR-5/RFC-9, replacing
ADR-2 for that scope. H7 inspection still shows immediate generation only. Please
track the implementation gap and resolve P4's timing criteria before reporting
verified behavior.” This preserves decision authority and asks for concrete follow-up.

Practice: add a deployed candidate H8 with a queue worker and a successful bounded
large-export check. Implementation evidence becomes stronger for that checked
candidate and path. It still cannot satisfy an undefined timing target. If the
local merge-rule row is removed instead, merging RFC-9 no longer independently
establishes acceptance; ADR-5's explicitly accepted state remains a separate record.

### 4) **Common confusion**

A rationale is not a proof that the decision will work. It explains why the choice
was made under assumptions that may later change. Likewise, a decision record is
not necessarily an implementation plan, and a PRD is not necessarily a frozen
contract. Establish the project's actual purpose and lifecycle for each artifact.

“Superseded” does not mean historically false. An old record can correctly explain
why an earlier release behaves as it does while being inappropriate guidance for
a new implementation. Keep both time and scope visible.

### 5) **Performance note**

Document names have no direct runtime effect. A decision can propose a queue to
change waiting and resource use, but the ADR supplies rationale, not a performance
result. Replacing immediate generation can also change user-visible completion
semantics. No document merge, queue implementation, deployment or timing experiment
was performed here; H7 and H8 are constructed evidence variations.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to mark three independent facts: current decision, inspected
candidate and measured outcome. Then remove the merge rule. They should narrow
the RFC inference while retaining ADR-5's explicitly supplied state. This tests
source and authority reading rather than memorizing document abbreviations.
