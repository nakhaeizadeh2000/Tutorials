## 6.2. Builds releases deployments rollouts rollbacks and feature flags

A new image can be running in production while its new report screen remains
hidden. Conversely, changing a feature flag can expose behavior without installing
a new image. “We released it” needs a subject: an artifact, a deployment or a
user-visible capability.

This lesson uses fictional delivery records. It performs no deployment or change
to an application's users, flags or stored data.

### 1) **Separate artifact preparation placement and user exposure**

A **build** prepares software outputs from specified inputs. Its detailed meaning
belongs to the earlier build/check lesson. A successful build alone does not show
that its artifact was installed or that users can reach it.

A **deployment** places a software version or configuration into a specified
operating environment. State the target: staging and production deployments are
both deployments. A completed placement record still needs its declared completion
criteria; copying files is weaker evidence than a verified running-version record.

**Release** is overloaded. A package publisher may release an archive for download;
a product team may release a feature by making it available to users. Some teams
use release for the whole production delivery process. Declare the local meaning.
Here, **feature release** means exposing the changed capability to its intended
users. **Rollout** means the process of introducing a change across its intended
instances or user population; it can be gradual or all at once.

A **feature flag**, also called a **feature toggle**, selects behavior through
configuration or contextual decisions. One deployed artifact may contain both
old and new paths. A **cohort** is a defined group receiving a particular treatment,
such as internal reviewers or selected accounts. Explain whether a percentage
refers to users, requests, instances or something else; those denominators differ.

Pete Hodgson's original [feature-toggle explanation](https://martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html)
separates deployment from feature exposure and distinguishes release, experiment,
operational and permission-oriented uses. A flag can provide a **kill switch**
for a particular behavior, but its actual control points determine what it stops.
A hidden button does not establish server-side authorization or stop a background
worker using the same feature. Use the existing access-control owner for those rules.

Flag changes can themselves require a deployment, restart or propagation interval.
Do not assume an immediate, globally consistent switch. Record who changes the
setting, how applications observe it and which paths were checked. Give temporary
flags an owner and removal condition: leaving obsolete branches indefinitely adds
configuration and testing work without preserving a useful decision.

### 2) **Compare rollout strategies and scope recovery actions**

These names describe different aspects of introducing a change:

| Term | Core idea | Question that the name leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Rolling update | Replace subsets of old instances with new ones over time | Can old and new instances interoperate while both are serving? |
| Canary release or deployment | Expose a limited population to a change and evaluate it before proceeding | Which population, signals, observation period and stop decision are used? |
| Blue-green deployment | Prepare an alternate environment and switch the relevant traffic between environments | Which components and data are separate or shared, and can traffic safely switch back? |

A rolling update is not automatically a canary evaluation. Kubernetes documents
[gradual Pod replacement](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/controllers/deployment/)
under its RollingUpdate strategy. That mechanism does not by itself establish a
comparison of customer outcomes. Google's original [canarying chapter](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
adds evaluation and its integration into the rollout decision. A small first step
with no meaningful observation can miss a defect before the wider rollout proceeds.

A canary may use routing to a new version or flags within one version. Its limited
population can reduce exposure to some defects, but shared databases and external
side effects can affect people outside the directly selected group. The **blast
radius** is the scope of impact of a failure or change; it needs a stated boundary,
not an assumption that a 5% traffic split limits every possible harm to 5%.

In blue-green arrangements, the colors are labels. They do not require a particular
cloud provider or two wholly independent systems. Fowler's [blue-green overview](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/BlueGreenDeployment.html)
describes switchable environments and the difficulty of database changes. Sharing
one database can preserve common data access while also preserving a common failure
dependency. Having two application environments does not prove disaster recovery.

A **rollback** attempts to return a defined part of the system to an earlier state.
Specify whether it restores an application version, configuration, routing or data.
A **roll forward** introduces another change intended to correct the problem. Neither
name guarantees successful recovery. Choosing an older application image does not
undo already completed emails, payments or data transformations. An earlier reader
may also be incompatible with data written by the newer version. Plan the actual
compatibility and recovery boundaries rather than assume a switch restores the past.

### 3) **Read an exposure timeline without inventing a recovery result**

Use this **constructed packet**. For this team's report feature, release means
that an account can use the new behavior; deployment means the image is running.
The flag controls only interactive report generation.

| Time | Supplied record |
|---|---|
| 09:00 | Build B12 produced image D12 containing old and new report paths |
| 09:20 | Running-version inventory shows D12 on every production report instance; the new-path flag is off for all accounts |
| 09:30 | Effective flag evaluations enable the new path for 20 of 1,000 named accounts; other accounts use the old path |
| 09:40 | Evaluation finds wrong totals on the new path and pauses wider exposure; the background export worker is outside this flag |
| 09:45 | Effective evaluations and checked interactive requests show all accounts back on the old path; images remain D12 |
| Recovery note | D12's startup migration removed a field that image D11 requires; no compatible D11 data-recovery procedure is supplied |

At 09:20, D12 is deployed, but this team's new feature has not been released to
accounts. At 09:30, the exposure fraction is 20/1,000 = 2% of accounts. It is not
necessarily 2% of requests or compute. Because this limited exposure is evaluated
before expansion, it is a canary arrangement under the supplied process.

At 09:45, the checked interactive exposure has been reversed through the flag;
there has been no image rollback. The record does not show that the background
worker stopped, that earlier incorrect exports were repaired or that every user
workflow recovered. The D11 field dependency also means “just use D11” lacks a
supported compatibility and recovery path. It does not prove that recovery is
impossible; it identifies the missing procedure and evidence.

Practice: remove the effective evaluations and checked requests at 09:45, leaving
only “operator saved off in the flag console.” What changes? The intended setting
is recorded, but its adoption and actual behavior are unverified. Request those
observations before reporting that user exposure has ended.

### 4) **Common confusion**

Publishing a package, installing an image and enabling a capability can happen at
different times. A Git tag is not evidence for all three. A canary's small rollout
step and its evaluation are separate ingredients, while a rolling update primarily
describes replacement over time.

A flag-off action and an image rollback also have different scopes. Either can
leave prior side effects and shared data untouched. Name the behavior restored and
the observations supporting that statement.

### 5) **Performance note**

Parallel old/new capacity can consume extra resources during blue-green or rolling
changes. Flag evaluation can also add local work or remote dependencies, depending
on implementation. A 2% account cohort supplies no workload or latency estimate.
Compare relevant outcomes by version or treatment so an aggregate does not hide a
small group's failures. This packet contains no performance measurement or measured
recovery time.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Hide the time column's labels and ask the learner to identify when bytes, placement,
exposure and observations change. Then ask what “rolled back” could truthfully mean
at 09:45. A sound answer retains D12, scopes the checked interactive behavior and
asks about the worker, previous outputs and D11 data compatibility.
