## 1.2. Auth, identity, secrets, and least privilege

### 1) **Authentication proves who, authorization decides what**

**Definitions** — *Authentication* (AuthN) verifies identity (passwords, keys, SSO, passkeys — "are you who you claim?"). *Authorization* (AuthZ) grants permissions to that identity (roles, policies — "may you do this?"). *Identity* is the account/principal both attach to — human, service, or machine.

- **What problem it solves**: conflating the two misdiagnoses every access failure — locked-out users get their *roles* re-examined while the real fault is an expired *credential*, and intrusions get read as "hacked passwords" when the weakness was over-broad *permissions* on a valid account. Separate words, separate fixes.
- **Modern guidance** — HTTP already encodes the split: 401 = authenticate (unknown identity — log in!); 403 = authorized-against (known identity, denied — stop asking or escalate properly). Say "authN failure" vs "authZ denial" in tickets and the fix owner is obvious before the second sentence.

```text
# Bad: auth-blur (wrong team investigates)
"Access denied on /admin — reset the user's password."

# Good: AuthN vs AuthZ split (owner obvious)
"Login succeeds (AuthN fine, SSO trace attached); /admin
 returns 403 (AuthZ denial — missing admin role grant).
 Fix: role assignment, not credential reset."
```

### 2) **Secrets are credentials that must never leak**

*Secrets* — passwords, API keys, private keys, tokens — authenticate holders to systems. *Vaults* (secret managers) store them encrypted with access audit; *rotation* replaces them on schedule or suspicion; *leak* means exposure (repo, log, screenshot) demanding immediate revocation, not quiet deletion.

- **Why it hurts (breaches, money)**: leaked cloud keys get harvested by bots within minutes — real bills (crypto-mining, exfiltration) accrue while teams debate whether the commit "counts". A secret in git history is compromised forever (history is forever); deleting the file changes nothing until the credential is revoked and rotated.
- **Modern guidance** — three verbs, no exceptions: *vault* (never env-files-in-repos, never chat-pasted), *rotate* (scheduled + on departure/suspicion), *revoke-first-on-leak* (invalidate, then clean history, then postmortem). Prefer short-lived credentials (OIDC, workload identity) over long-lived keys — secrets that expire themselves can't leak usefully.

### 3) **Least privilege limits blast radius**

*Least privilege* grants each identity the minimum access its job needs — nothing more, no "just in case admin". *Blast radius* is the damage one compromised identity can cause; every excess permission enlarges it in advance, for the attacker's convenience.

- **What problem it solves**: breaches are inevitable; blast radius is a choice. Read-only deploy keys, per-service database users, and time-boxed admin elevation convert "total compromise" into "one small room" — the difference between a bad day and a company-ending event is usually decided years earlier, in permission grants nobody reviewed.
- **Modern guidance** — default-deny with explicit grants, review access quarterly (departures, role changes, stale service accounts), and make elevation temporary + audited (break-glass with expiry, not standing admin). "Why does this need write?" asked at grant time is free; asked during incident response is priceless.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "SSO means we're secure" — SSO centralizes *authentication*; authorization sprawl (every app's role model) and secret hygiene remain — one solved third of the problem.
- "Internal tools don't need auth" — insider threats, compromised laptops, and SSRF turn "internal" into "attacker-adjacent"; authenticate everything, authorize narrowly, everywhere.
- "Rotating secrets breaks things" — rotation breaks things *once*, then automation; never rotating breaks things catastrophically, once, at 3 AM, during a breach.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Audit one junior's first access grant together: walk identity → needed actions → minimal scope → expiry. Fifteen minutes of "why does this need write?" installs least-privilege thinking permanently — and publish the team's standing-access inventory quarterly, because permissions nobody remembers granting are permissions nobody will miss revoking.
