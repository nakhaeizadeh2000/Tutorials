## 2.2. On-call, runbook, hotfix, and rollback words

### 1) **On-call owns the pager**

**Definition** — *On-call* is the rotation that owns production response: one named human (primary + secondary) carries the pager for a bounded shift, acknowledges alerts within minutes, and *escalates* (pulls in deeper expertise) when the fix exceeds their reach. *Paging* interrupts a human; *ticketing* queues work for later — severity decides which.

- **What problem it solves**: unnamed ownership means alerts wake everyone or no one — diffusion of responsibility at 3 AM. The rotation converts "somebody should look" into a name, a number, and a minutes-scale SLA, which is the entire difference between response and bystanding.
- **Modern guidance** — keep rotations humane (follow-the-sun or ≤1 week in 4, compensated, with post-incident rest), escalate early without stigma (calling for help at minute 10 beats heroics at hour 3), and page only on user-impacting symptoms (per [2.1](<./2.1. Pipeline observe and incident words.md>) — actionable alerts or silence).

```text
# Bad: ownership-free (everyone and no one responds)
"Prod is erroring — can someone take a look??"
 [4 people read it, 0 acknowledge, 25 min pass]

# Good: rotation-owned (name, ack, escalate)
"SEV1 declared, paging primary (Lena). Ack in 3 min,
 investigating checkout 500s. Escalated DBA at +12 min
 for lock analysis. Updates every 15 min in #inc-412."
```

### 2) **Runbook scripts the response**

A *runbook* (playbook) is the written procedure for a known failure class: symptoms → diagnosis steps → fix commands → escalation triggers. Good runbooks read like checklists under stress; bad ones read like essays nobody opens during fires.

- **Why it matters (speed, safety)**: incidents degrade thinking — the runbook externalizes it. "Disk-full on db-primary: confirm with X, clear WAL with Y, escalate if replication lag > Z" resolves in minutes by anyone on rotation; without it, each responder re-derives the procedure while users wait, and improvised fixes cause the secondary outages postmortems are famous for.
- **Modern guidance** — write runbooks from real incidents (postmortem action item #1), test them in game days, and keep them executable (copy-paste commands, expected outputs, explicit escalation lines). A runbook nobody has rehearsed is documentation theater — schedule the rehearsal with the writing.

### 3) **Hotfix skips the line, rollback undoes**

A *hotfix* is an emergency forward fix — expedited review, abbreviated testing, direct-to-prod path — carrying explicit risk in exchange for speed. A *rollback* (*revert*, *roll back*) undoes a release — restoring the previous known-good state, ideally within minutes via flags or artifact promotion.

- **Why it matters (risk arithmetic)**: both are emergency tools with opposite profiles — hotfix gambles that the fix is right (fast if correct, compounding if wrong); rollback gambles nothing (known-good state, certain outcome). Teams that hotfix by default stack risk on risk; teams that rollback first stabilize, then fix calmly forward.
- **Modern guidance** — rollback first, hotfix second: when prod burns, restore known-good in minutes, *then* craft the forward fix through the normal pipeline. Precondition: every release must be reversible (flag flip, prior artifact ready) — per the Domain 03 rule, reversibility is release-process quality, verified before merge, not during fire. Hotfixes still go through post-deploy verification and a next-day postmortem.

### 4) **Common confusion**

- "On-call means always available" — it means available *in rotation*, with boundaries, compensation, and rest; permanent availability is a staffing failure wearing dedication's clothes.
- "Runbooks replace thinking" — they replace *recall* under stress; novel failures still need diagnosis — runbooks buy calm minutes for the thinking that matters.
- "Rollback means failure" — rollback means the *safety system worked*; stigmatizing it incentivizes hotfix gambling, which fails louder.

### 5) **Mentor note**

Review every incident's first 15 minutes, not just its root cause: who was paged, how fast the ack, when escalation happened, whether a runbook existed. Response mechanics improve faster than systems do — and juniors who watch their ack-times trend down learn that operations is a practiced skill, not a talent for heroics.
