## 4.1. UI UX usability accessibility and responsive design

A booking screen looks tidy on a phone, but its date picker cannot be operated
with a keyboard and its confirmation message leaves users unsure whether a room
was reserved. Calling the whole screen “good UI/UX” hides the specific successes
and failures. Use vocabulary that names the user, task and barrier.

The examples here are constructed descriptions. This is not an accessibility
audit of a real product, a design implementation or a conformance certificate.

### 1) **Distinguish the interface from the experience and task success**

**UI** means **user interface**: the controls, content and feedback through which
a person interacts with a system. It includes what an action does and how the
result is communicated, not just colors and spacing. A text command interface is
also a UI; a UI need not be graphical.

**UX** means **user experience**: the person's wider experience of using and dealing
with the product or service. It includes finding the right capability, completing
the workflow and understanding what happens afterward. A polished booking screen
cannot compensate for discovering only after submission that the room cannot be
booked through this service. Don Norman and Jakob Nielsen's original
[UX explanation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/definition-user-experience/)
distinguishes interface quality from this wider experience.

**Usability** concerns how effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily particular
people can accomplish particular goals in a context. **Effectiveness** asks whether
the intended outcome is achieved; **efficiency** asks about the effort and resources
needed; **satisfaction** concerns the person's experience of use. A workflow can
be possible but unnecessarily hard to learn or recover from after a mistake.
The [WAI discussion of usability and accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/fundamentals/accessibility-usability-inclusion/)
explains their overlapping concerns.

For the booking example, “three screens” is an interface count. “New staff can
choose an available room and correctly recognize confirmation without assistance”
is an outcome to investigate. Fewer screens are not automatically better if the
change hides availability, omits an essential choice or makes errors difficult to
repair. State the relevant user and task before judging ease of use.

**Usability testing** observes representative people attempting representative
tasks. It can reveal a misleading instruction or an unexpected recovery problem.
Jakob Nielsen's [usability introduction](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-101-introduction-to-usability/)
describes this task-observation approach. A team member saying “I like it” is a
preference report, not equivalent evidence that intended users complete the task.

### 2) **Recognize accessibility barriers and the scope of standards**

**Accessibility** concerns enabling people with disabilities to perceive,
understand, navigate and operate the experience. Disabilities and access needs
are diverse; do not reduce accessibility to blindness, text size or a single
screen-reader check. The [W3C WAI introduction](https://www.w3.org/WAI/fundamentals/accessibility-intro/)
explains this focus and its benefits in varied situations.

**Assistive technology** supports access, for example a screen reader presenting
content through speech or braille, or software supporting alternative input.
A **screen reader** does not simply describe a screenshot: the program needs usable
information about content, controls and their state. **Keyboard focus** identifies
the current target for keyboard interaction; its visual indicator helps a user
locate that target. A control that looks present can still be unreachable or
unidentifiable through another interaction method.

For example, a date picker that offers its only selection mechanism through mouse
clicks creates a barrier for someone using a keyboard interface. A field labeled
only by an unexplained icon can create another barrier. **Accessible name** means
the programmatically available name used to identify a control; a visible caption
and an accessible name need to be implemented coherently. These terms describe
information and behavior, not a request to add arbitrary extra labels everywhere.

**WCAG** expands to **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines**. WCAG 2.2 provides
testable success criteria grouped under perceivable, operable, understandable and
robust principles, with conformance levels A, AA and AAA. A **success criterion**
is a particular condition to assess. For example,
[WCAG 2.2 criterion 2.1.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#keyboard)
addresses operation through a keyboard interface, including its stated exception
for underlying path-dependent movement. A standard's exact criterion matters;
a slogan such as “supports accessibility” is too vague to establish conformance.

An **accessibility evaluation** combines appropriate checks and knowledgeable
judgment against a stated scope. Automated tools can identify some issues, but
passing one scan does not establish that a workflow is accessible or that all
applicable criteria were assessed. Standards and involvement of people with
disabilities contribute different evidence; neither an isolated user session nor
a checklist alone covers every possible need. Keep the evaluation's limits visible.

### 3) **Separate responsive layout from usable interaction**

**Responsive web design**, often **RWD**, adapts presentation to different available
sizes and conditions. A **viewport** is the browser area available for presenting
the page, not necessarily the entire physical screen. A **breakpoint** is a condition
where layout rules change. Flexible layouts can also adapt continuously between
such conditions. [MDN's responsive-design guide](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Core/CSS_layout/Responsive_Design)
introduces these ideas without requiring a separate mobile site.

The original [Responsive Web Design](https://alistapart.com/article/responsive-web-design/)
article by Ethan Marcotte motivates adapting one experience to different media.
Today's layout tools go beyond that article's historical examples. The useful
vocabulary is adaptation to available space; it does not require reproducing a
2010 technique or targeting only two named device sizes.

A narrow screenshot can show that content fits at that captured size. It cannot
show that a keyboard user can reach the calendar, a zoomed view retains essential
content or a confirmation is understandable. **Responsive** can also mean quick
to react in a performance discussion. Clarify “responsive layout” versus “responsive
to input” when the context is ambiguous.

Apply the distinctions to this **fictional evidence card**:

| Evidence | Supported conclusion | Still unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| Fields stack into one column at a tested narrow viewport | A layout adaptation exists for that condition | Other sizes, zoom and the complete interaction |
| Keyboard user cannot choose a date using the only date selector | A task-blocking input barrier is reported | The implementation cause and effectiveness of a repair |
| Confirmation says only Done; users ask whether the booking exists | Feedback is ambiguous for those users | The actual server result and a clearer validated message |
| One automated accessibility scan reports no findings | That tool reported no findings in its scanned scope | Unchecked criteria and actual task completion |

The answer is to retain the successful layout observation while investigating the
separate barriers. Propose keyboard-operable date selection and feedback tied to
the actual reservation result, then verify the complete task. Do not mark the
experience accessible because the screenshot is neat, or classify every problem
as visual styling. Changing layout alone does not supply missing behavior or
clarify an uncertain application outcome.

### 4) **Common confusion**

UI and UX are related, not interchangeable. The UI is a major part of the
experience; service capability, content and what happens after submission matter
too. Accessibility and usability overlap, but a study that excludes people with
disabilities may miss important access barriers.

Accessibility is also not a special mode that can simply be switched on after
every other decision. Content meaning, interaction and feedback need to work
together. Conversely, one reported barrier does not reveal every defect or prove
the cause before the relevant behavior has been investigated.

### 5) **Performance note**

Task efficiency and runtime speed are different measurements. Fewer confusing
steps may reduce human effort without changing response time; faster server
processing may still leave an inaccessible control unusable. Layout adaptation
alone guarantees neither a smaller transfer nor faster input handling. This card
contains no task-time or rendering measurement, and its vocabulary labels do not
establish a speed improvement.

### 6) **Mentor note**

Ask the learner to turn “make the UI responsive and accessible” into separate
acceptance questions for layout, keyboard selection and confirmation. A strong
answer names the task and observation for each, identifies the evaluation scope
and refuses to infer successful booking from a message alone. Then ask which
parts a static screenshot can and cannot establish.
