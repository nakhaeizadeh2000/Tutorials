# 2.1. Capacity modeling with measured triggers

## 1. Growth rates are business facts (rows per day per table — asked, not guessed)

**Definition** — capacity modeling starts with one question per table, answered by the business (not the database): "how many rows per day, growing how fast?" Orders/day from sales forecasts, users/day from growth plans, events/day from product instrumentation roadmaps. Rates are business facts with sources (forecast doc, analytics trend, launch plan) — recorded with the source and date, because sources expire (forecasts revise, launches slip, virality surprises) and expired rates silently invalidate every projection built on them.

**What problem it solves** — the guessed-growth duo: *under-provisioned optimism* ("a few thousand a day" — actually 200k/day after launch — storage fills in months, indexes outgrow RAM, the 3 a.m. disk-full page nobody modeled) and *over-provisioned anxiety* ("millions, probably" — actually thousands — partitioned/sharded complexity adopted for volume that never arrives, operated forever). Both come from unasked questions — growth rates known approximately by the business (they plan hiring, support, and revenue on the same numbers) but never requested by engineering. Asking converts both failures into arithmetic.

**Modern guidance** — the growth inventory per feature (five columns, one row per table): *table, rows/day today, growth rate (%/mo or launches), source, as-of date*. Sources ranked (measured production > analytics trend > sales forecast > founder estimate — precision descends, stated honestly). Revisit triggers (launch events, pricing changes, new channels — anything invalidating the source re-opens the row). "Unknown" is an acceptable rate with an expiry ("unknown — launch in 6 weeks, re-measure week 2" — bounded ignorance with a re-ask date, not permanent fog). No feature ships its schema without the inventory — the review gate from [4.1](<../4. Mentor checklist/4.1. Evolution review checklist mentors insist on.md>).

```sql
-- GOOD: the growth inventory — rates sourced, dated, revisit-triggered (bookstore example).
-- orders: 50k/day today (measured prod, 2026-09) +8%/mo (sales forecast Q4) → 100k/day by Q2.
-- order_lines: 150k/day (3.0 lines/order measured) — tracks orders × multiplier.
-- events: 2M/day (instrumentation roadmap — 5 new event types H1, rate revises then).
-- UNKNOWN with expiry: returns (feature unlaunched — re-measure 2 weeks post-launch).
-- Triggers: pricing change → re-ask sales; new channel → re-ask growth; all rates re-read yearly.
```

```sql
-- BAD: growth by adjective — unasked, unsourced, unusable.
-- "Orders: lots (it's a store). Events: many (tracking everything). Users: hopefully millions."
-- Storage sized by vibes (2TB "should be enough" — until events 10× in a quarter);
-- indexes assumed resident (working set 3× RAM by month 8 — p99 cliff, unmodeled);
-- partitions "considered later" (later arriving as emergency re-partitioning under load).
-- Adjectives aren't arithmetic; capacity needs numbers with sources and dates.
```

**Common confusion** — *"capacity planning is ops' job."* Operations provisions what modeling predicts — unmodeled growth arrives in ops as emergencies (disk-full pages, surprise sharding projects, emergency archival). The modeler knows the shapes (row widths, index counts, access patterns); ops knows the hardware (disk, RAM, IOPS budgets). Capacity modeling is the *interface* between them — owned jointly, initiated by whoever sees growth first (usually the modeler, holding the forecast conversation). "Not my job" ships the emergency to whoever's on call.

**Performance note** — growth rates are performance inputs in disguise: rows/day × days = table size (index depth, scan cost, vacuum load — all size functions); writes/sec = contention surface (lock rates, WAL volume, replica lag — all rate functions). Unmodeled growth degrades performance invisibly (p99 creeping as indexes deepen — attributed to "database slowness," fixed by tuning what modeling should have sized). Modeled growth schedules responses (index maintenance windows, partition splits, archival — calendared, boring). Boring capacity is successful capacity.

**Mentor note** — teach "ask the business for numbers" as a junior superpower (juniors fear stakeholder conversations; growth questions are the easiest entry — businesspeople *love* sharing forecasts, and engineers asking signal seriousness). Role-play once ("how many orders next quarter? source?") — juniors discover forecasts exist and are shared freely. The skill transfers to every estimation task thereafter (API quotas, cache sizes, team hiring — all start with "ask for the rate"). Estimators are made, not born — made by asking.

## 2. Storage math per row (width × count × indexes × overhead — the headroom spreadsheet)

**Definition** — the capacity spreadsheet: per table, row width (measured, not estimated — `pg_total_relation_size` ÷ rows on production-like data, verified below), times projected count (rates × horizon — 12–18 months), times index multiplier (heap + indexes — indexes often 1–3× heap for indexed-heavy tables), times overhead (TOAST for wide values, bloat headroom ~20%, WAL/replica multiplication for write-heavy). The product is storage-per-table over the horizon — summed across tables, compared against disk/RAM budgets, with headroom thresholds triggering responses (next promise).

**What problem it solves** — the width-blind estimate ("10M rows — small!" — at 4KB/row with 3× indexes = 160GB — not small; at 200B/row = 8GB — genuinely small). Row counts without widths are meaningless (two tables, same count, 20× different storage); heap without indexes understates by multiples (the "data fits" claim ignoring that indexes must *also* fit — in RAM, for performance, not just on disk); projections without overhead expire on first bloat/vacuum cycle. Width × count × indexes × overhead — all four factors, measured where possible, estimated with margins where not.

**Modern guidance** — the measurement procedure (quarterly, automated where feasible): (a) *width*: `pg_total_relation_size(table)/count` on production or production-like staging (verified pattern below — heap + toast + indexes per row, one number); (b) *count projection*: rates (promise 1) × horizon months; (c) *index multiplier*: `pg_indexes_size/pg_relation_size` ratio today, assumed stable barring new indexes (new-index proposals update the multiplier — index review includes capacity impact); (d) *overhead*: +20% bloat/vacuum headroom, ×replicas for WAL-proportional costs on write-heavy. Spreadsheet per table, summed per database, reviewed against budgets — the capacity review from [4.1](<../4. Mentor checklist/4.1. Evolution review checklist mentors insist on.md>).

```sql
-- GOOD: measured width, projected count, multiplied honestly (verified PG 16.15, dbprobe).
-- 10k sample rows: heap + indexes measured (pg_total_relation_size pattern below).
SELECT pg_size_pretty(pg_total_relation_size('users')) AS total,   -- heap+toast+indexes
       pg_size_pretty(pg_relation_size('users')) AS heap,
       pg_size_pretty(pg_indexes_size('users')) AS idx;
-- Spreadsheet: width 420B/row measured × 50k/day × 540 days (18mo) = 11.3B rows... wait:
-- 50k/day × 540 = 27M rows × 420B = 11.3GB heap × 2.4 idx-multiplier = 27GB × 1.2 = 33GB.
-- Verdict: fits (500GB budget, 7% — headroom comfortable; trigger at 60%: re-model).
```

```sql
-- BAD: count-only arithmetic — widths unmeasured, indexes ignored, overhead absent.
-- "27M rows — fine, rows are small." (420B measured — smallish, but ×2.4 indexes ×1.2
-- overhead = 3× the naive guess. At 4KB widths (JSONB payloads unmeasured): 27M × 4KB
-- = 108GB × 3 = 324GB — 65% of budget, trigger tripped on arrival.) Counts without
-- widths are hopes; hopes aren't capacity plans. Measure the row, multiply honestly.
```

**Common confusion** — *"storage is cheap — why model it?"* Disk cheap, RAM dear, and performance lives in RAM (working-set residency — indexes + hot heap in memory — is the p99 cliff; disk-full is merely the outage). Capacity modeling prices *RAM residency* (will hot indexes fit in 18 months?) more than disk totals — "storage is cheap" answers the disk question nobody asked while the RAM question pages at 3 a.m. Model both; fear RAM; respect disk.

**Performance note** — the residency cliff: hot working set exceeding RAM turns indexed lookups into disk reads (p99 steps 10–100× at the cliff edge — sudden, not gradual — attributed to "mystery slowness" until someone plots set-vs-RAM). The spreadsheet predicts the cliff date (set size ÷ growth = months remaining — calendared response: archival, partitioning, RAM upgrade — chosen calmly). Unmodeled cliffs arrive as incidents; modeled cliffs arrive as tickets. Same physics, different on-call experience — modeling is incident prevention wearing arithmetic.

**Mentor note** — teach `pg_total_relation_size` as the "weigh the table" reflex (juniors estimating storage from column types learn measurement beats multiplication — TOAST, alignment, null bitmaps, and index bloat defy armchair math). One measured table converts estimators to measurers permanently ("weighed 420B/row, not the 200B I guessed" — humility via catalog functions). Weighing is a five-minute query; guessing is a quarters-long liability. Weigh, don't guess — the track's oldest lesson, storage edition.

## 3. Triggers turn anxiety into runbooks (partition, archive, reprice at numbers — not feelings)

**Definition** — capacity triggers: pre-committed (metric, threshold, response, owner) tuples that convert growth from ambient worry into scheduled action — "orders table >100M rows → range-partition by month (owner: platform, procedure: Domain 05 §2.2)"; "events older than 13 months → cold archive (owner: data, procedure: §3.1)"; "working set >70% RAM → archival sprint (owner: team, procedure: capacity review)". Triggers are monitored (dashboards, alerts) and calendared (reviewed quarterly whether firing or not) — the track's standing trigger discipline ([Domain 02 §4.1](<../../../02 Normalization Deep Dive/sections/4. Mentor checklist/4.1. Normalization review checklist mentors insist on.md>), [Domain 04 §3.1](<../../../04 Relationships at Scale/sections/3. Skew and hot relationships/3.1. Hot keys and skew.md>)) applied to growth quantities.

**What problem it solves** — the two capacity failure modes: *untriggered anxiety* ("we should partition someday" — someday never scheduled; the table hits 500M rows unpartitioned; emergency re-partitioning under load with downtime negotiations) and *untriggered complacency* ("plenty of headroom" — headroom consumed silently; disk-full pages during the holiday freeze; RAM cliff crossed mid-incident). Triggers pre-commit both the recognition (metric crosses threshold — alert fires, no human judgment required at 3 a.m.) and the response (procedure named — runbook exists, rehearsed or referenced). Worry without triggers is rumination; headroom without triggers is luck.

**Modern guidance** — the trigger format, one line per growth risk: *metric* (rows, GB, RAM%, age — queryable, dashboarded) → *threshold* (number with margin — fires before pain, not during: 70% not 95%) → *response* (procedure named — partition/archive/upgrade with section links) → *owner* (team/person — paged when firing, reviewing quarterly). Thresholds set from the spreadsheet (promise 2's projections — "100M rows in 14 months at current rate → threshold 80M, fires month 11, response window 3 months"). Responses rehearsed where expensive (partition-split runbook tested on staging — untested runbooks are documentation, not capability). Quarterly trigger review (firing? approaching? obsolete?) — calendared, never "when someone remembers."

```sql
-- GOOD: triggers as data — metrics, thresholds, responses, owners (bookstore set).
-- orders_rows > 80M → range-partition by placed_at month (procedure 2.2; owner platform).
-- events_age > 13mo → cold archive (procedure 3.1; owner data; legal signed retention).
-- working_set_RAM_pct > 70% → archival sprint + RAM quote (owner team; review monthly).
-- Dashboard: three gauges, three alerts, zero ambiguity. Fires → runbook, not meeting.
-- Quarterly: thresholds re-derived from fresh rates (rates expire — promise 1).
```

```sql
-- BAD: capacity intentions — worry and headroom, neither instrumented.
-- "We'll partition orders when it gets big." (Big = ? Measured where? Who decides?
-- The table hits 400M rows; "big" arrived unannounced; emergency partitioning negotiated
-- with downtime during peak season.) "Events have plenty of retention." (Policy? Job?
-- Legal signed? The answers are "no," discovered during the GDPR audit — findings,
-- fines, and a purge project under regulatory deadline.) Intentions aren't triggers;
-- unmeasured thresholds never fire; unfired thresholds are hopes with dashboards missing.
```

**Common confusion** — *"triggers are ops monitoring, not modeling."* Monitoring watches metrics; modeling *chooses* them (which quantities, which thresholds, which responses — all design decisions: partition key from 2.2, archival tiers from 3.1, spreadsheet from promise 2). Unmodeled monitoring alerts on symptoms ("disk 90%") with no procedure; modeled triggers fire on causes ("rows 80M") with runbooks. The modeler designs the trigger set; ops operates it — interface again ([promise 1](#1-growth-rates-are-business-facts-rows-per-day-per-table--asked-not-guessed)'s joint ownership). Design the alerts you'd want to receive — then receive them calmly.

**Performance note** — trigger thresholds carry margins for response latency (fire at 70% with 3-month response window — not 95% with a weekend): partitioning 100M rows takes hours-to-days (planning, rehearsal, execution, verification); archival of billions takes weeks (legal sign-off, cold-write verification, purge batching); RAM upgrades take procurement cycles (quotes, approvals, maintenance windows). Threshold = pain-point minus response-lead-time in growth units — computed from rates (promise 1), not chosen round. Round thresholds ("100M, nice number") with unpriced responses are hopes with significant figures.

**Mentor note** — teach "what fires, who runs, by when?" per trigger (the three questions converting worry to runbook — juniors answering precisely design operations, not just schemas). Review triggers quarterly *with the owners present* (firing soon? procedure still valid? owner still correct?) — stale triggers (wrong owner, obsolete procedure, threshold passed silently) are worse than none (false confidence — "we have triggers" covering decayed ones). Calendar the review; audit the triggers; trust only firing-tested paths. Boring capacity reviews are successful capacity reviews.
