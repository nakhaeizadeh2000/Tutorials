# 5.1. Common interview QA normalization

## 1. Normalize on a whiteboard (the dependency-first narration that shows method)

**Definition** — the exercise: *"here's a flat table (orders with customer city, instructor per enrollment…) — normalize it."* The graded artifact is narration through dependencies, not the final boxes: arrows first (`customer_id → city`), anomaly named ("update anomaly on move"), split justified ("city lives with its determiner"), form named last ("that's 3NF"). Numbers without arrows are recitation; arrows without numbers still pass.

**What problem it solves** — candidates either freeze (memorized definitions, no procedure) or over-split (5NF everything from anxiety — [2.1's stopping rule](<../2. The core ladder/2.1. Second and third normal form.md>) violated live). Interviewers probe normalization because it reveals whether design is *procedural* (dependencies → anomalies → splits) or *decorative* (buzzwords arranged confidently). Method narrated out loud is unmistakable and unfakeable.

**Modern guidance** — the five-minute shape: (1) *arrows* (1 min — list dependencies from the business, asking "can X have two Ys?" out loud); (2) *anomaly* (1 min — narrate one concrete failure: "customer moves, N rows update, partial failure contradicts"); (3) *split* (2 min — move the attribute to its determiner's table, state keys); (4) *name and stop* (1 min — "that's 3NF; BCNF vacuous here, no overlapping keys — stopping is correct"). Step 4's restraint is graded heaviest: interviewers watch what you *don't* split.

```
-- GOOD (whiteboard, orders with customer_city — 5 min narrated):
-- "Dependencies first: order_id → customer_id; customer_id → city. Chain through
--  a non-key column — transitive. Anomaly: customer moves → N order rows update;
--  crash mid-way → two cities for one customer (which ships?). Split: customers
--  table holds city, orders keeps customer_id FK. That's 3NF. Stopping: single-column
--  surrogate keys, no overlap — no BCNF defect to chase. Done — and I'd record the
--  gap: minimum-cardinality (orders per customer ≥ 0 fine) needs no action here."
```

```
-- BAD (whiteboard): silent splitting, then "it's normalized" — or the anxious climb.
-- (Boxes redrawn without a spoken dependency; or: "...and then I'd check BCNF, 4NF,
--  5NF..." on a two-table bookstore — formalism without a defect, time evaporating.)
```

**Common confusion** — *"I should name every normal form to show knowledge."* Naming without diagnosing shows recall; diagnosing without naming shows judgment — interviews hire judgment. One correctly-narrated 3NF split with a stated stopping reason beats a tour of all five forms with no anomaly named. Depth in one place, not breadth everywhere.

**Performance note** — if asked "doesn't normalization slow queries?" (the inevitable follow-up), answer with the join shape: "splits join on PK lookups — microseconds; the anomaly removed was O(N) writes. I'd take the join and measure." Then offer the priced exit: "hot paths get deliberate copies ([3.1](<../3. Judgment/3.1. Deliberate denormalization with priced trade-offs.md>)'s discipline) — after measuring." Measurement mentioned twice in one answer: hired.

**Mentor note** — drill with a timer and grade narration separately from schema (same method as [Domain 01 §5](<../../../01 Modeling Foundations and Mental Model/sections/5. Interview QA/5.1. Common interview QA modeling foundations.md>)): dependencies stated? Anomaly narrated with a crash scenario? Stopping reason given? Candidates reliably fix narration faster than knowledge — three timed rounds transform ramblers into narrators.

## 2. The denormalization judgment call ("when would you break 3NF?" — the trade-off answer)

**Definition** — the senior filter: *"when would you denormalize?"* The failing answers are absolute ("never — integrity first" freezes real dashboards; "for performance" prices nothing). The passing answer is a *priced story*: a specific hot path, measured numbers, the chosen copy shape, the consistency mechanism, the staleness contract, and the revisit trigger — [3.1's six fields](<../3. Judgment/3.1. Deliberate denormalization with priced trade-offs.md>) compressed to ninety seconds.

**What problem it solves** — interviewers ask this because production demands it weekly and juniors answer it tribally ("my last team always…"). The question separates engineers who *choose* redundancy (trade-off with ledger) from engineers who *inherit* it (habit without pricing). The ledger is the whole signal — mechanism details are secondary and follow-up-able.

**Modern guidance** — the ninety-second shape: (1) normalize-first credo (one sentence — "the truth stays 3NF so copies stay rebuildable"); (2) the measured pain ("dashboard p95 2.1s at 50k orders/day"); (3) the copy ("materialized view, CONCURRENTLY, 5-min refresh"); (4) the contract ("dashboard-only, never finance — staleness stated, not discovered"); (5) the trigger ("revisit at 1M/day — then synchronous or drop"). Close by naming what you *wouldn't* copy (OLTP write paths — rent exceeds savings). Five beats, no rambling, numbers included.

```
-- GOOD (spoken, 90 seconds):
-- "Normalize first — copies need rebuildable truth. Then: our dashboard aggregated
--  order lines per page load, p95 2.1 seconds at 50k orders a day. Copy: materialized
--  view of per-order totals, refreshed CONCURRENTLY every 5 minutes — p95 to 40ms.
--  Contract: dashboard only, finance reads lines — 5-minute staleness stated in the
--  record (ADR-021), never discovered. Revisit at a million orders a day or on finance
--  adoption — then trigger-maintained or dropped. I wouldn't copy on the write path:
--  rent on every insert, savings on few reads — ratio says no."
```

```
-- BAD (spoken): the unpriced absolute — correct instinct, zero engineering.
-- "I wouldn't denormalize — it risks inconsistency." (Dashboard still p95 2.1s.
-- The interviewer hears: this candidate ships slow dashboards rather than pricing
-- trade-offs. Or the mirror: "For performance, obviously." No numbers, no contract,
-- no trigger — this candidate ships the next fossilized total column.)
```

**Common confusion** — *"the interviewer wants a yes/no."* They want a *ledger*: costs, mechanisms, contracts, triggers. "It depends" with six priced fields is an answer; "never" and "for performance" are postures. Senior interviews grade bookkeeping over bravery — show the books.

**Performance note** — volunteer the read:write ratio unprompted ("profitable because dashboards read 1000× per write — I'd refuse the same copy on the checkout path"). Ratios generalize across every denormalization question ever asked; candidates who reason in ratios answer variants (caches, counters, rollups) without new preparation.

**Mentor note** — rehearse this as storytelling, not recitation: a real incident (or realistic composite) with numbers, a decision, and an outcome. Stories with numbers stick in interviewers' scorecards; abstractions evaporate. One priced war story, rehearsed to ninety seconds, answers this question and its three most common follow-ups.

## 3. Anomalies as war stories (update, insert, and delete anomalies through production consequences)

**Definition** — the recall question behind the war story: *"what's an update/insert/delete anomaly?"* The junior answer defines; the senior answer *narrates*: a production-shaped incident per anomaly, with consequence attached — contradiction shipped, onboarding blocked, history lost. Definitions prove study; stories prove the model lives in how you think about outages.

**What problem it solves** — anomaly questions filter for experience-with-consequences: anyone can memorize "update anomaly = redundancy"; few connect it to "two cities on invoice vs profile, support ticket, manual reconciliation." Interviewers promote candidates who already think in incidents — because production will supply them, and pre-loaded patterns diagnose faster. Stories are incident-response pre-training made audible.

**Modern guidance** — one story per anomaly, thirty seconds each, same schema (orders/customers/city — reuse keeps focus on consequences, not setup): **update** — "customer moved, backfill updated 2M of 2.1M rows before crashing — invoices shipped from two cities for a week; fix: split + backfill + UNIQUE, copy dropped"; **insert** — "new corporate customer, no orders yet — onboarding stalled three days waiting for a schema exception because the city had nowhere to live without a fake order"; **delete** — "GDPR-adjacent purge deleted the last order of a dormant customer — their verified address (needed for tax records) went with it; restored from backup, then split." Consequence → root cause → fix, every time.

```
-- GOOD (spoken, 90 seconds total — consequence first, jargon second):
-- "Update: a move updated 2M of 2.1M order rows before the job died — two cities
--  for one customer, invoices wrong for a week. That's the update anomaly: one fact,
--  N homes. Insert: onboarding stalled — a customer with no orders couldn't record
--  a city without a fake order; the schema forbade a legal business state. Delete:
--  purging the last order destroyed the address tax needed — the delete anomaly
--  as silent data loss. All three: one dependency misplaced. Fix all three: one split."
```

```
-- BAD (spoken): the glossary — correct, forgettable, unhired.
-- "An update anomaly is when redundant data is updated inconsistently. An insert
--  anomaly is when insertion is prevented by missing data. A delete anomaly is when
--  deletion loses unintended data." (True, complete, and indistinguishable from ten
--  other candidates — no consequence, no fix, no signal.)
```

**Common confusion** — *"war stories require real incidents."* Constructed stories from real patterns count — interviewers grade causal structure (redundancy → trigger → consequence → fix), not employment history. The schema is real, the mechanism is real, the numbers are plausible: that is a war story with the names filed off, and it signals identically.

**Performance note** — close any anomaly story with the write-amplification footnote ("N-row updates became one row — the split was a performance fix wearing a correctness costume"). Interviewers noting performance awareness alongside correctness promote faster — correctness gets you hired, cost-awareness gets you leveled.

**Mentor note** — build the team's story bank: every anomaly-class incident becomes a thirty-second narrative in the bank, told to juniors within a month. Story banks compound — three stories teach this domain's *why* better than any training budget, and juniors who inherit stories tell them in interviews. Institutional memory as hiring advantage.
