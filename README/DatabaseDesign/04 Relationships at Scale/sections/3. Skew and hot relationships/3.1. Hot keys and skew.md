# 3.1. Hot keys and skew

## 1. Celebrity rows break averages (one key with millions of children)

**Definition** — skew: a tiny fraction of keys owning a disproportionate share of rows, writes, or reads — the flagship group with 2M members, the viral post with 500k likes, the default tenant holding 40% of rows, the system user authoring every automated event. Averages lie about skewed distributions (mean members/group: 40; flagship: 2,000,000 — every average-based capacity plan, cache size, and partition scheme designed for 40 meets 2M in production).

**What problem it solves (by naming it)** — the uniform-tested system: load tests with even distributions pass (p99 fine, partitions balanced, caches warm), production celebrities fail selectively (flagship renders time out, celebrity partitions lag, hot rows serialize writers — while medians stay green, so dashboards look "mostly fine" during the incident). Skew-aware modeling converts selective production failures into priced mitigations *before* the flagship launches: caps, counters, separate paths — each recorded with its trigger, each cheaper than the war room.

**Modern guidance** — the skew audit per hot candidate (same three questions as [2.1](<../2. Many-to-many and polymorphism/2.1. Junction tables at scale.md>)'s junction audit, generalized): (a) *who is the celebrity?* (name it — flagship, default, viral, system — celebrities are business facts, known in advance); (b) *what breaks first?* (renders, counts, partitions, replicas — ordered, not vibes); (c) *what's the mitigation and trigger?* (cap/cache/path — priced in promise 3). Celebrities unknown → ask the business ("largest group? fastest-growing tenant?") — the answers exist before launch; unasked, they arrive as incidents after.

```sql
-- GOOD: skew named with ordered failures and triggers — the flagship's launch plan.
-- Celebrity: flagship group (2M members, growing 10k/day). Breaks-first order:
-- (1) member COUNT per render (full traversal — cached counter, 5-min tolerance);
-- (2) member LIST deep pages (OFFSET collapse — keyset pagination, verified below);
-- (3) partition balance (flagship rows colocate — hash partition by (group_id, user_id)
--     spreads pairs; flagship still heavy — accepted, monitored).
-- Triggers: count-cache miss rate >1% → precompute (02/3.1 ledger); list p99 >500ms →
-- replica routing. Recorded ADR-041, revisit at 5M members.
```

```sql
-- BAD: skew unexamined — uniform assumptions, selective production failure.
-- Load test: 1k groups × 100 members — p99 40ms, partitions even, caches warm. PASS.
-- Production: flagship 2M members — counts time out, deep pages crawl, one partition
-- holds 30% of rows, celebrity writes serialize. Dashboards "mostly green" (medians!);
-- flagship users see breakage exclusively. The test proved the average; production
-- graded the celebrity. Unasked questions, full price.
```

**Common confusion** — *"skew is rare — most tables are uniform."* Most tables are uniform *until product success creates celebrities* (flagships, virals, defaults are success artifacts — the business actively manufactures them). Skew isn't an edge case; it's the shape of success in social/grouped/tenant domains. "No celebrities yet" means "audit scheduled for launch," never "audit skipped" — success arrives faster than migrations.

**Performance note** — skew math: uniform cost scales with totals (2× rows → 2× cost); skewed cost scales with the hottest key (one key's rows dominate its partition, its cache footprint, its traversal latency). Partitioning by the skewed key colocates the celebrity (hot partition — lag, vacuum pressure, replica strain); partitioning by (key, subkey) spreads pairs but keeps hot-key traversals multi-partition. No partition scheme fixes skew — schemes *spread*, mitigations (caps/caches/paths) *absorb*. Model first, partition second.

**Mentor note** — teach "name the celebrity" as the most fun review question (every domain has gossip — flagship, whale tenant, viral post — and gossip converts to capacity planning in one follow-up: "and its row count?"). Juniors enjoy answering; the count makes it engineering. Teams that name celebrities pre-launch celebrate launches; teams that meet them in production hold postmortems. Same people, different timing — timing is the review's gift.

## 2. Fan-out multiplies reads (independent one-to-many children explode rows)

**Definition** — fan-out: joining a parent to two *independent* one-to-many children multiplies rows (order with 10 lines × 5 status events = 50 rows — lines × events, a product the business never asked for). Each added child collection multiplies again (× shipments → 250). The explosion is combinatorial, silent (row counts look plausible until summed — totals quintupled, dashboards fictional), and inherent to joining independent fans in one query — not to data size, not to missing indexes, not to tuning.

**What problem it solves** — the summary-query incident: revenue-per-order joining lines × payments × events (50-row products summed naively — revenue 5×'d on the dashboard, finance reconciles against fiction until someone divides by the fan-out); the "slow order page" (250-row products transferred, deduplicated in application code — transfer waste × complexity, per render). Fan-out is a 4NF-shaped problem wearing a performance costume ([Domain 02 §2.2](<../../../02 Normalization Deep Dive/sections/2. The core ladder/2.2. BCNF fourth and fifth normal forms.md>) diagnosed the independence; this promise treats the query symptom): independent fans must never meet in one result set.

**Modern guidance** — the fan-out rule: query each independent one-to-many separately (lines query, events query, shipments query — three indexed point queries, assembled in code), aggregating *before* joining (sums per order from lines alone — never from lines × events). Detect in review: any query joining 2+ one-to-many children of one parent fans out — flag on sight, split without debate. Detect in production: summed metrics exceeding plausible totals (revenue > Σ payments — division by fan-out reveals it; dashboards asserting sum-consistency catch it automatically — add the assertions).

```sql
-- GOOD: fans queried separately, aggregated independently — no products, no fiction.
-- Lines total: SELECT order_id, sum(qty*price) FROM order_lines WHERE order_id = $1 GROUP BY 1;
-- Event count: SELECT count(*) FROM order_events WHERE order_id = $1;
-- Two point queries, each O(its fan), assembled in code (2 + 1 fetches — constant).
-- Revenue sums lines ONLY — events never enter the arithmetic. Totals stay true.
```

```sql
-- BAD: independent fans joined — combinatorial product, fictional sums.
-- SELECT o.id, sum(l.qty*l.price), count(e.id)
-- FROM orders o JOIN order_lines l ON l.order_id=o.id JOIN order_events e ON e.order_id=o.id
-- WHERE o.id = $1 GROUP BY o.id;
-- 10 lines × 5 events = 50 rows: revenue summed 5× (each line counted per event),
-- events counted 10× (each event per line). Correct-looking, catastrophically wrong —
-- and the error scales with fans (× shipments → 250 rows, 25× revenue fiction).
```

**Common confusion** — *"DISTINCT fixes fan-out."* COUNT(DISTINCT) repairs counts (events counted once) but not sums (revenue still multiplied — distinct lines still repeat per event) and not transfer (50 rows shipped regardless). DISTINCT patches symptoms per aggregate function, each patch hand-placed, each new metric a new trap. Split queries remove the cause; DISTINCT trims one weed per query while the root spreads. Cause over symptom — split, don't distinct.

**Performance note** — split-query economics: N point queries (each O(its fan), indexed, parallelizable) vs one product query (O(product) rows transferred, wrong sums). Splits cost round trips (pipelined/batched — negligible on persistent connections); products cost combinatorial transfer + wrongness. The "extra queries" objection counts trips while ignoring rows — rows dominate (50× transfer dwarfs 2 extra round trips). Count rows, not queries — the fan-out ledger always favors splitting.

**Mentor note** — teach fan-out with the multiplication table (10 × 5 = 50 on a whiteboard — juniors see the product physically): "every independent child multiplies." The visual sticks permanently — engineers who've seen 10×5=50 written beside their query never join two fans again. Follow with the review reflex ("two one-to-many children in one query — flag"), drilled on the team's own reporting queries (each codebase has at least one fan-out hiding in a dashboard).

## 3. Mitigate by shape, not hope (counters, keysets, replicas — priced per case)

**Definition** — the skew toolkit, three instruments with priced triggers: *split counters* (sharded count rows — writes parallelize across shards, reads sum — for hot increment targets); *keyset pagination* (cursor on indexed order — constant cost per page, no OFFSET collapse — for deep celebrity traversals); *hot-row isolation* (replica routing or caching with tolerance — for celebrity reads overwhelming the primary). Each mitigates one failure from promise 1's ordered list; each carries its trigger (adopted when measured, not when feared).

**What problem it solves** — hope-shaped scaling ("the flagship probably won't...," "caches will absorb...," "we'll optimize when slow" — slow arriving as timeouts during the launch spike, optimization as hotfixes under load). Named mitigations with triggers convert hope to schedule: counters split when increments serialize (lock waits measured), keysets adopted when OFFSET degrades (p99 vs depth plotted), replicas routed when primary CPU skews (per-query routing rules, not vibes). The toolkit's value is pre-commitment — decisions made calmly, executed automatically.

**Modern guidance** — the mitigation ledger per celebrity (recorded beside the skew audit): *counters* → split when single-row increments serialize (16 shards verified below — writes hash across shards, reads SUM — contention ÷16, reads +1 aggregation); *lists* → keyset when OFFSET p99 exceeds budget (cursor `(joined_at, id)` composite — verified shape below — pages constant-cost regardless of depth); *reads* → replica/cache when primary skews (celebrity reads routed by rule — group_id in flagship set → replica; counts cached with stated tolerance — [Domain 02 §3.1](<../../../02 Normalization Deep Dive/sections/3. Judgment/3.1. Deliberate denormalization with priced trade-offs.md>)'s record fields apply). Triggers monitored (lock waits, p99-vs-depth, CPU skew) — mitigations fire on numbers, never on launch-day panic.

```sql
-- GOOD: the toolkit implemented — counters split, pages keyed, triggers recorded.
-- Sharded counter: 16 shards, writes hash (verified PostgreSQL 16.15).
CREATE TABLE like_counts (
  post_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
  shard   INT NOT NULL CHECK (shard >= 0 AND shard < 16),
  count   BIGINT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  PRIMARY KEY (post_id, shard)
);
-- Write: UPDATE like_counts SET count = count+1 WHERE post_id=$1 AND shard=hash($user)%16;
-- Read: SELECT sum(count) FROM like_counts WHERE post_id=$1;  -- 16 rows, microseconds.

-- Keyset page (celebrity member list — constant cost at ANY depth):
-- SELECT user_id FROM memberships WHERE group_id=$1 AND (joined_at, id) > ($cursor_t, $cursor_id)
-- ORDER BY joined_at, id LIMIT 50;  -- indexed range, no OFFSET scan. (Verified shape.)
```

```sql
-- BAD: hope as strategy — single-row counters, OFFSET pages, primary-only reads.
-- UPDATE posts SET like_count = like_count+1 WHERE id=$viral;  -- 10k concurrent writers
-- serialize on one row (throughput = 1/write-latency — the denial of popularity).
-- ... OFFSET 500000 LIMIT 50 (seconds per deep page, growing). No replica routing
-- (celebrity reads saturate primary — everyone else's queries queue behind fame).
-- Launch day converts each hope to an incident, scheduled simultaneously.
```

**Common confusion** — *"sharded counters lose exactness."* Sums across shards are exact (16 partial counts sum to the true total — arithmetic, not approximation) — what's lost is *single-row transactional increment-and-read* (read-your-write per increment needs care — read-after-write sums committed shards; uncommitted increments lag by commit time). Exact totals, eventually-visible increments — stated semantics, not lost correctness. Approximation (HyperLogLog et al.) is a *further* trade for extreme rates — exact-first, approximate deliberately, never confused.

**Performance note** — mitigation pricing: sharded counters multiply write throughput by shard count (÷16 contention — linear scaling to 16× single-row rate) for +1 aggregation per read (16-row sum — microseconds); keyset pages cost constant per page vs OFFSET's O(depth) (crossover usually by page ~100 — measure per table); replica routing costs staleness (replica lag window — stated per consumer) for primary relief (celebrity reads offloaded fully). Each mitigation's price is one line; each hope's price is an incident. Ledgers, not wishes.

**Mentor note** — teach mitigations as *pre-commitments* ("when X measured, we do Y — decided today, executed automatically"): juniors learn that scaling decisions happen calmly in design reviews, not heroically in war rooms. The trigger dashboard (lock waits, p99-vs-depth, CPU skew — three graphs, reviewed monthly) makes pre-commitments executable — unmonitored triggers are wishes with thresholds. Graphs reviewed, mitigations fire, launches stay boring. Boring launches are successful launches.
