# 5.1. Common interview QA modeling foundations

## 1. Surrogate versus natural under pressure (the five-minute answer that shows judgment)

**Definition** — the interview question is some variant of *"would you use email as the primary key of users?"* The answer they grade is not yes/no — it is the change-story: what happens when the value changes, who pays, and what you default to instead ([2.1](<../2.%20Identity%20and%20keys/2.1.%20Surrogate%20versus%20natural%20keys.md>) is the full treatment; this is its five-minute performance).

**What problem it solves** — candidates recite "surrogate keys good" with no reasoning, or defend naturals with sample data ("emails are unique!"). Both fail the senior bar. Interviewers probe key choice because it reveals whether you think in *lifecycles* (what changes) or *snapshots* (what the sample shows) — the single most predictive modeling signal.

**Modern guidance** — the narrated answer, in order: (1) default ("surrogate BIGINT, natural values UNIQUE beside it"); (2) the change-story ("emails change → one UPDATE with surrogates, cascade-rewrite without"); (3) the exception ("true naturals: ISO codes, fixed standards — stable for decades"); (4) the scale footnote ("narrow keys keep FK indexes small"). Ninety seconds. Then stop — over-answering signals insecurity, and the interviewer will probe where they want depth.

```sql
-- GOOD (whiteboard): write the default AND the exception — shows range, not dogma.
users (id BIGINT PK, email TEXT UNIQUE NOT NULL)   -- "default: surrogate"
currencies (code CHAR(3) PK)                        -- "exception: ISO standard, decades-stable"
-- Narrate: "emails change and merge; currency codes change on decade timescales
-- with global announcements — different change-rates, different keys."
```

```sql
-- BAD (whiteboard): the dogmatic answer — correct default, zero reasoning, no exception.
users (id BIGINT PK, email TEXT UNIQUE NOT NULL)
-- "Surrogate keys are best practice."  -- why? "Best practice." The interview ends here.
```

**Common confusion** — *"interviews want the textbook answer."* Junior loops might; senior loops want the *trade*. "It depends" without a default is evasion; a default without a change-story is recitation. Default + story + exception is the senior shape for every design question, not just keys.

**Performance note** — if pressed on performance ("why not UUID everywhere?"), place the scheme on 2.2's axes (generation × locality) out loud: "UUIDv4 buys distributed minting, pays B-tree scatter and 16-byte keys — right for offline writers, wrong for single-primary." Naming both axes under pressure is the expert signal.

**Mentor note** — drill this as a 90-second timed answer, then critique the *narration*, not the content: did the change-story come before the exception? Did they stop at the footnote? Interview performance is rhetoric over knowledge — candidates who know 2.1 cold still fail by rambling. Time them.

## 2. Design a schema on a whiteboard (the nouns-verbs-constraints method, narrated out loud)

**Definition** — the exercise: *"design a schema for X"* (bookstore, URL shortener, ride-sharing). The graded artifact is the *narration*, not the boxes: thinking out loud through nouns → verbs → constraints ([3.1](<../3.%20First%20design%20moves/3.1.%20From%20requirements%20to%20tables.md>) compressed to fifteen minutes) while the interviewer watches decisions happen.

**What problem it solves** — silent whiteboarding fails even with a perfect schema: the interviewer cannot grade decisions they never heard. Conversely, narrated imperfection passes: "I'm making orders→customer NOT NULL because guest checkout is out of scope — flagging that assumption" shows scoping, judgment, and communication in one sentence.

**Modern guidance** — the fifteen-minute shape: (1) *clarify scope* (2 min — "guest checkout in scope? refunds?"): questions before boxes; (2) *nouns* (3 min — entities with keys, surrogate default stated once); (3) *verbs* (5 min — relationships with cardinalities, junction where N-to-N, hidden entities named); (4) *constraints* (3 min — UNIQUEs, NOT NULLs, one CHECK to show you think in rules); (5) *gaps + scale* (2 min — "minimum-cardinality unenforced, owned by app; hot table is X"). Narrate assumptions as you go; write the gap list unprompted — it is the senior tell.

```
-- GOOD (whiteboard narration, URL shortener — 15 min):
-- "Scope first: custom aliases? expiry? analytics? [asks, listens]
--  Nouns: urls (surrogate id, short_code UNIQUE, target NOT NULL, expiry nullable),
--         clicks — hidden entity in 'track visits' (lifecycle: recorded, aggregated).
--  Verbs: a url has many clicks; clicks never move → FK NOT NULL, CASCADE on url delete?
--         [asks] — retention policy decides; default RESTRICT, purge job later.
--  Constraints: short_code UNIQUE (the business identity), target CHECK (valid URL shape —
--         app validates, CHECK bounds). Gap: click-fraud dedup is app-side (IP+window).
--  Scale: clicks dwarf urls — hot table, FK indexed, partition-ready by month (Domain 05)."
```

```
-- BAD (whiteboard): silent boxes, then "done" — ungradeable decisions, unasked scope.
┌──────┐  ┌──────┐
│ urls │──│clicks│   -- (no keys shown, no cardinalities, no constraints,
└──────┘  └──────┘    no scope questions, no narration — the interviewer learned nothing)
```

**Common confusion** — *"I should finish the schema."* Completeness is ungraded; prioritization is. A half-finished schema with stated assumptions, a gap list, and a scale note beats a complete silent one every time — because production design works exactly that way (time-boxed, assumption-laden, revisited).

**Performance note** — the scale note (2 min, step 5) is disproportionately graded: naming the hot table and its growth driver ("clicks grow per-visit, urls per-user — three orders apart") shows you model for operation, not just correctness. One sentence; senior signal.

**Mentor note** — mock this monthly for juniors seeking senior roles: rotate domains (commerce, social, logistics) so they practice *method*, not memorized schemas. Grade narration quality (assumptions stated? gaps listed? stopped on time?) separately from schema quality — the former predicts offers better.

## 3. What breaks at ten times the data (identity, cardinality, and constraint answers that scale)

**Definition** — the follow-up: *"your design works — now 10x the data/tenants/traffic. What breaks first?"* The graded skill is *ordered* failure prediction: name the first bottleneck, the second, and what you would have modeled differently knowing scale was coming — without redesigning everything on the spot.

**What problem it solves** — designers defend day-one shapes ("it handles current load"); seniors are hired for year-three shapes. The 10x question separates modeling that *happens to work* from modeling with *headroom priced in* — cardinality bets revisited (1.2), key strategies stressed (2.2), constraints audited for contention.

**Modern guidance** — answer in failure order, three deep: (1) *identity* ("sequences hold to ~10k writes/s single-primary; past that, UUIDv7 or sharded sequences — migration priced in ADR"); (2) *cardinality* ("the 1-1 profile photo becomes a gallery — separate table already, one INSERT not a migration; the orders→lines hot center grows fastest — partition-ready"); (3) *constraints* ("UNIQUE on short_code contends under concurrent creation — retry-on-conflict owned by app; FK checks stay cheap, indexed"). Close with what you'd change *today* given the knowledge (usually: one table split, one key decision) — restraint graded over redesign.

```sql
-- GOOD (narrated 10x on the bookstore): ordered, priced, restrained.
-- "First: order_lines volume — 10x orders × lines; FKs indexed already, range-partition
--  by placed_at when (not now — measured trigger: 100M rows). Second: checkout contention
--  on inventory counts — today's CHECK can't serialize decrements; row-lock or queue,
--  app-side (Databases 04 mechanics). Third: idempotency_key UNIQUE stays the hero —
--  retries grow with traffic, constraint scales free. Today's change: snapshot unit_price
--  onto lines NOW (G2 from the gap list) — price-history rewrites at 10x are the migration
--  I refuse to run later."
```

```sql
-- BAD: scale theater — buzzwords, no order, no prices, redesign everything.
-- "We'd shard... everything? Microservices? NoSQL for the hot paths?"
-- (No named first bottleneck, no measured trigger, no priced migration —
--  the answer costs more than the problem.)
```

**Common confusion** — *"scale answers mean distributed systems."* At 10x, answers are still modeling: split tables, key changes, partition readiness, constraint contention. Sharding/regions are 100x answers — reaching for them at 10x signals resume-driven design. Interviewers asking 10x want headroom thinking, not infrastructure theater.

**Performance note** — every 10x answer needs a *measured trigger* ("partition at 100M rows," "UUIDv7 at 10k writes/s") — triggers turn anxiety into runbooks. Untriggered scale plans are procrastination in the other direction (premature work instead of premature worry).

**Mentor note** — teach the "three deep, then stop" discipline: first bottleneck, second, today's one change. Juniors either name one ("it'll be fine") or twenty (panic redesign). Three ordered failures with triggers is the senior shape — drill it until it's reflex, because production incidents demand exactly this ordering under worse pressure.
